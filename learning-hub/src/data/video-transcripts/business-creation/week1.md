### PART 2: LESSON — Why Most Businesses Fail

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Here is a question I want you to sit with: why do most businesses fail?

It is not because the founders were lazy. It is not because they lacked passion. It is not because their product was terrible. Most businesses fail for one reason, and it is the same reason every time: the founder assumed they already knew what customers wanted — and never tested that assumption before building the whole thing.

[PAUSE]

Michael Gerber, in his book The E-Myth Revisited, tells a story that might sound familiar. A woman named Sarah loves baking pies. She is brilliant at it. Her friends tell her so constantly. One day, exhausted by her office job and intoxicated by the dream of independence, she decides to open a bakery. Within two years, she is out of business.

Not because she could not bake. She could bake magnificently. She failed because she never stopped to ask whether loving to bake and knowing how to run a bakery business are the same thing.

They are not. They are almost completely different skill sets.

[GRAPHIC: Text overlay — "The Fatal Assumption: The belief that because you understand the technical work of a business, you understand the business that does that technical work." — Michael Gerber]

[ON CAMERA]

Gerber called this the Fatal Assumption. And it does not just kill bakeries. It kills restaurants, consultancies, handmade goods businesses, tech startups, and social enterprises with equal enthusiasm. The woodworker who opens a furniture shop. The programmer who launches an app. The agronomist who starts a consulting firm. The pattern is always the same: passion for a craft, confidence that others will value it, the decision to build based on that confidence alone — and then the slow discovery that customers do not behave the way the founder imagined.

[PAUSE]

What makes this pattern so persistent is that the Fatal Assumption feels completely reasonable. You have real skills. You have real experience. Your friends love your product. Your colleagues respect your expertise. But here is the hard truth: friends are not customers. Colleagues are not a market. The people who tell you your idea is great are almost never the ones who will open their wallets.

[B-ROLL: Someone showing a product to enthusiastic friends who nod and smile, then cut to the same person sitting alone in an empty shop]

[ON CAMERA]

The fatal moment is not launching a bad idea. It is launching without first discovering whether the idea is good.

Now, the traditional response to this problem was the business plan. You know the kind — a thick document forecasting revenues, describing the competitive landscape, outlining marketing strategies, projecting three to five years into the future with impressive spreadsheets. Banks love business plans. Governments grant them weight. Entrepreneurship textbooks teach you to write them.

The problem? A business plan, however detailed, is a work of fiction. It is a document about what you believe will happen, written before you have tested whether any of it is true.

Steve Blank, the Stanford professor who helped develop the lean startup approach, put it bluntly: "No business plan survives first contact with a customer."

[PAUSE]

Now, this is not a criticism of planning. Planning matters. Strategy matters. But there is a critical difference between a plan — which describes what you intend to do — and a hypothesis — which describes what you believe to be true and that you are about to test.

Think about that word: hypothesis. It comes from science. A hypothesis says: "I believe X is true. Here is how I will find out whether X is actually true. Here is what I will do if X turns out to be false." It gives you permission to be wrong and to learn from being wrong. And that — learning from being wrong — is the only path to eventually being right.

[GRAPHIC: Side-by-side comparison:
"Business Plan: X is true. Here is what we will do."
"Business Hypothesis: I believe X is true. Here is how I will test it."]

[ON CAMERA]

The businesses that survive are almost always the ones that began with an honest acknowledgment of what they did not know. The founders treated their earliest assumptions not as facts but as experiments. They moved quickly, learned fast, spent little, and adjusted constantly. This approach has a name — the Lean Startup, developed by Eric Ries. And while the term was coined in Silicon Valley, the logic applies just as powerfully to a craftsperson in Bishkek, a textile seller in Tashkent, or a food vendor in Almaty.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Here is the promise of this course: you do not have to repeat the fatal pattern. You do not have to bet your savings, your family's stability, and years of your life on assumptions you have never tested. You can start smaller, learn faster, and build something that works because you built it around what customers actually need.

[SERIOUS TONE]

This is not the comfortable path. Testing your assumptions honestly often means discovering they were wrong. That can sting. But it is infinitely better to discover you were wrong after a week of testing than after a year of building.

This week, we begin with honesty. Specifically, the honest work of mapping every assumption hiding inside your business idea and asking, with genuine openness: what if I am wrong about this?

That question, answered rigorously and honestly, is the foundation of a business that survives.

[PAUSE]

Before we wrap up today, I want to leave you with this warning — and I mean it as a warning, not just a suggestion.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "The most dangerous words in entrepreneurship are 'I know my customers.' You may know people like your customers. You may know what you wish they wanted. Knowing what your customers actually need requires listening to them — before you build."]

[ON CAMERA]

Think about this before tomorrow's lesson: What is the one thing you are most confident customers will love about your idea? And — honestly — how do you know that is true? Have you asked them? Or have you assumed?

I will see you tomorrow. We are going to talk about the engine that drives every successful business: Build, Measure, Learn.

## Day 2: Build-Measure-Learn — The Engine of Every Successful Business

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome back. Yesterday we talked about the Fatal Assumption — the dangerous belief that you already know what customers want before testing it. Today, we are going to talk about what to do instead. This is the most important framework you will learn in the entire course.

It is called Build-Measure-Learn, and it is the engine that drives every successful lean business.

[PAUSE]

Let me tell you a story. Imagine a cafe owner named Nurlan in Bishkek. He is convinced that what the city's business district needs is a premium third-wave coffee experience — single-origin beans, pour-over method, minimalist design, prices that reflect the quality.

He spends six months finding the right space, importing equipment from Germany, training his staff, designing a menu, building out the interior. He opens. The first week is slow. The second week is slower. By month three, he is quietly alarmed. His customers are spending an average of ninety minutes at a table, ordering one drink, and using his Wi-Fi. His premium pricing is keeping out the office workers who could have been his lunch rush.

The product is excellent. The market response is wrong.

[B-ROLL: A quiet, beautifully designed cafe with only one or two customers sitting with laptops]

[ON CAMERA]

What Nurlan did is completely understandable. And extremely expensive. He optimized for a hypothesis he never tested. He assumed premium coffee was the gap in the market, that business-district workers would pay premium prices, that fast table turnover would support his margin model, and that local cafe culture would match what he had seen in Istanbul and Berlin.

Each of those assumptions was partially wrong. Discovering that cost him six months of rent, imported equipment he could not sell, and a customer base that had already formed habits elsewhere.

[PAUSE]

The Build-Measure-Learn loop exists to prevent exactly this scenario.

[GRAPHIC: Circular diagram showing three connected stages:
BUILD (smallest possible version) → MEASURE (real customer response) → LEARN (confirm or adjust) → back to BUILD]

[ON CAMERA]

The concept is simple. Instead of building everything at once based on your assumptions, you build the smallest possible version of your idea that will let you test your most critical assumption. You call this a Minimum Viable Product, or MVP.

Then you measure how real customers respond — not in surveys, not in theory, but in actual behavior. Do they buy? Do they return? Do they tell other people? Do they pay the price you expected?

And then, based on what you learned, you either confirm your hypothesis and build further, or you adjust and test again.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a real example that shows how this works naturally, even without knowing the term.

[B-ROLL: Someone photographing handmade items and posting them on Instagram]

[ON CAMERA]

A Kazakh craftsperson making traditional felt goods — let us call her Aigul — starts by posting photos of three designs on Instagram. She does not make fifty items in advance. She makes three, posts them, and waits to see which designs people actually respond to. Not just with heart reactions — but with "how much?" and "can I order one?"

She learns that one design draws almost no response while another generates five inquiries before she has even finished listing it. She scales the successful design first.

She is running Build-Measure-Learn without a business school degree. Her limited resources forced her to learn before she spent. And that is the entire philosophy.

[PAUSE]

Now, the measure step is where most aspiring entrepreneurs get uncomfortable. Measuring means defining, in advance, what success looks like. It means saying: "I will know my hypothesis is correct when I see X."

For Nurlan, a valid early metric might have been: "I will know business-district workers will pay my prices if at least thirty percent of walk-in visitors order a drink without asking about the price."

For Aigul, it was simpler: "I will know which design is worth scaling if it receives at least three serious purchase inquiries in the first week."

Without pre-defining what "it is working" looks like, you are left interpreting ambiguous data through the distorted lens of hope.

[SERIOUS TONE]

And that leads me to the learn step. This is not merely absorbing what happened. It is actively updating your beliefs based on evidence. And this requires intellectual honesty that runs against human nature.

We are wired to confirm what we already believe. When Nurlan's cafe is slow in week one, the tempting interpretation is "people just need more time to discover us." And maybe that is true. But an honest interpretation asks: is the slowness evidence that my pricing assumption was wrong? That my location assumption was wrong? That my target customer assumption was wrong?

Learning means following the evidence wherever it leads, not using it to justify what you already believed.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Here is a story I want you to remember.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "A young entrepreneur in Almaty wanted to start a home-cooked meal delivery service. Instead of renting a kitchen and hiring delivery staff, she posted a message in a neighborhood WhatsApp group: 'I'm thinking of offering home-cooked Kazakh meals delivered for 1,500 KZT per portion. Who would be interested?' 23 people responded within an hour. She cooked 10 meals, delivered them on foot, and had 8 repeat orders the following week. Total investment before first revenue: under 5,000 KZT. That is the Build-Measure-Learn loop."]

[ON CAMERA]

The power of this loop is not speed, though speed is a benefit. Its real power is that it replaces opinion with evidence. Every cycle through the loop makes you incrementally less wrong about the market, about your customers, and about your business model.

You will never achieve perfect knowledge. Markets change. Customers are complex. But the entrepreneur who has completed ten Build-Measure-Learn cycles is operating with dramatically better information than one who spent the same ten weeks writing a business plan. One person knows what they believe. The other knows what they have discovered.

[PAUSE]

This week, you will take your first step into this loop. You will not build a product. You will not write a plan. You will identify the single assumption your business idea most depends on — the one that, if wrong, would make the whole idea fall apart — and you will design the smallest possible test to find out whether it is true.

The goal is not to succeed. The goal is to learn.

[PAUSE]

Here are two questions to think about before tomorrow's lesson. First: what is the smallest test you could run this week to learn something real about your business idea? And second: how would you know — from observable behavior, not from what people say — that your idea is working?

Sit with those. I will see you tomorrow.

## Day 3: Assumptions, Hypotheses, and How to Tell the Difference

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome back. We have spent two days talking about why businesses fail and about the Build-Measure-Learn engine that prevents those failures. Today we are getting practical. Today is about the most important exercise in Week One: mapping your assumptions.

[PAUSE]

Every business idea is a bundle of assumptions. Most entrepreneurs know this in the abstract but have never actually sat down to identify each assumption by name. And here is the thing — assumptions hide in plain sight. They live inside phrases like "people will obviously want" and "the market clearly needs" and "of course customers will pay." They disguise themselves as common sense.

Uncovering them requires the discipline to question what feels self-evident.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a specific example. Suppose you want to start a business tutoring high school students in mathematics, operating out of your apartment in Bishkek. Seems simple, right?

Look more carefully.

[GRAPHIC: List appearing one by one:
1. Parents in your area actively want tutoring
2. They will pay for an independent tutor rather than asking a relative
3. They will trust a tutor they don't already know personally
4. Students are struggling with math specifically (not other subjects)
5. Your apartment is a viable location
6. You can produce results fast enough that parents see value
7. Parents want 1:1 tutoring, not group sessions]

[ON CAMERA]

Count those. At least seven assumptions, for what looked like a simple idea. And each one is a point of potential failure.

If parents prefer group tutoring — which many do, because it reduces cost per student — your one-on-one model fails. If trust networks are so strong in your community that parents only hire tutors known to the family, your marketing strategy fails. If the real problem is not math but study habits, your subject-matter expertise fails to address what parents are actually paying for.

None of these failure modes are obvious until you list the assumptions explicitly and examine each one.

[PAUSE]

So how do you turn an assumption into a hypothesis? It is a practice in structured humility.

An assumption says: "Parents will pay two thousand som per hour for tutoring."

A hypothesis says: "I believe parents will pay two thousand som per hour for tutoring. I will know this is true if I can find five families willing to commit to a trial session at that price before I have set up my full schedule."

[GRAPHIC: Side by side:
ASSUMPTION: "Parents will pay 2,000 som/hour."
HYPOTHESIS: "I believe parents will pay 2,000 som/hour. I will know this is true when I find 5 families who commit to a trial session at that price."]

[ON CAMERA]

The hypothesis has three components the assumption lacks: a clear belief, a defined metric, and a test. This structure forces you to decide in advance what "working" looks like — which prevents you from moving the goalposts after the fact.

[PAUSE]

Now, when mapping your assumptions, it helps to organize them into categories.

[GRAPHIC: Four categories appearing:
1. CUSTOMER ASSUMPTIONS — Who they are, what they want, how they behave, what they'll pay
2. PROBLEM ASSUMPTIONS — How severe and frequent is the problem you're solving?
3. SOLUTION ASSUMPTIONS — Is your approach better than the alternatives?
4. MARKET ASSUMPTIONS — How big is the opportunity? How competitive?]

[ON CAMERA]

Each category contains its own landmines. Customer assumptions are beliefs about who your customers are and what they will do. Problem assumptions are about whether the problem you are solving is actually painful enough that people will change their behavior to fix it. Solution assumptions are about whether your particular approach beats the alternatives the customer already uses. And market assumptions are about the size and competitiveness of the opportunity.

[PAUSE]

The next step — and this is critical — is ranking your assumptions by risk. The question is: if this assumption turns out to be false, how much does it damage the business?

A low-risk assumption, if wrong, is inconvenient. A high-risk assumption, if wrong, is fatal.

For most businesses, the highest-risk assumptions concern two things: whether the customer actually has the problem you think they have, and whether they experience that problem as urgent enough to change their behavior. Everything else — your solution, your pricing, your marketing — rests on those two foundational assumptions. They should be tested first.

[SERIOUS TONE]

There is a phrase I want you to carry with you throughout this entire course. It was coined by Steve Blank, and it is this: "Get out of the building."

[GRAPHIC: Large text — "Get Out of the Building" — Steve Blank]

[ON CAMERA]

It means going to where customers actually are — their homes, their markets, their workplaces, their daily routines — and observing them, talking to them, and learning from them directly. Every assumption you have about customers was formed inside the building. The evidence that will confirm or refute those assumptions lives outside the building.

No amount of research done at a desk, no matter how thorough, equals a single honest conversation with a real potential customer.

[PAUSE]

And here is a practical tip that can save you from wasting those conversations.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "The 'Mom Test' (from Rob Fitzpatrick's book): Ask potential customers about their life and problems, NOT about your idea. 'Do you think this would be useful?' always gets a polite yes. 'How do you currently handle X? How much time does it take? What's the most annoying part?' gets you truth."]

[ON CAMERA]

"Do you think this is a good idea?" is a useless question. Everyone will say yes to be polite. Instead, ask about their life. Ask about their problem. Ask how they handle it now. Ask what they have already tried. Do not mention your solution until the very end — and even then, only to ask if it sounds useful. The conversation is about them, not about you.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

One final note. The goal of assumption mapping is not to discourage you. It is to make you dangerous — in the best sense. A founder who asks sharp questions, tests fast, and builds on evidence rather than hope is in a vastly stronger position than one who does not know what they do not know.

You cannot address a problem you have not named. Name your assumptions. Rank them honestly. Then go find out which ones are true.

[PAUSE]

Here are your three questions for today. First: what is the single assumption your business idea cannot survive being wrong about? Second: which of your assumptions feel most obvious and self-evident? Those are exactly the ones most worth testing. And third: if you had to test your riskiest assumption in forty-eight hours with almost no money, how would you do it?

Tomorrow, we hear Aijan's story. And it is going to feel familiar — because she made the exact mistake we have been talking about all week.

She invested her savings: eight thousand som on containers and a thermal delivery bag. Five thousand on her first week of ingredients from Osh bazaar. Another two thousand on printed flyers she slipped under office doors.

[ANIMATION: A kitchen. Early morning. Aijan cooking plov, lagman, and salad. Fifteen portions lined up in containers. Then a clock showing noon. Only four containers are gone.]

[ON CAMERA]

She cooked her first batch on a Monday — plov, lagman, and a salad — enough for fifteen portions. By noon she had sold four.

She told herself it was first-week slowness. The second week she sold eight portions total. She added beshbarmak on Fridays because her father always said it was the most Kyrgyz of all dishes and surely people would want it.

The portions sat in their containers through the lunch hour. She brought them home and fed them to Temirlan.

[SERIOUS TONE]

The problems were everywhere, and she had not seen a single one.

[GRAPHIC: List appearing:
- Assumed office workers wanted traditional Kyrgyz food — never asked them
- Assumed a flyer under a door was enough to trust a stranger with lunch
- Assumed beshbarmak — eaten slowly, with hands — would work in a 20-minute office break

She was a skilled cook. She was a careful planner. She was completely unprepared for the one thing no plan can substitute for: the honest conversation with a real customer, before anything is built, about what they actually need.

## Day 5: Action Steps — The Assumption Mapper

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day Five. This is where we take everything we have learned this week and put it to work. Today I am going to walk you through the Assumption Mapper worksheet — the most important exercise of Week One — and then I will explain your real-world activity for this week.

By the end of today, you should have your business idea written down, your assumptions mapped, your riskiest assumption identified, and a plan to test it.

Let us get started.

[SCREEN SHARE: The Assumption Mapper worksheet, showing the first section — "Your Business Idea"]

[ON CAMERA]

First things first. I need you to write down your business idea in one or two sentences. Not a paragraph. Not a business plan. One or two clear sentences that describe what product or service you want to offer, to whom, and how.

If you cannot state it in two sentences, it may not be clear enough yet — and that is fine. Clarity is what this exercise produces.

Right below that, write down who your target customer is. Be specific. Not "everyone" — that is not a customer. I want something like "mothers in urban Bishkek aged twenty-five to forty who work full-time and need affordable weekday meal options." The more specific you are here, the more useful everything else becomes.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 2 — "Customer Assumptions"]

[ON CAMERA]

Now we move to the heart of the exercise: your assumptions. We are going to work through four categories, and I want you to write down at least two assumptions in each one.

Category one: customer assumptions. What do you believe about who your customers are and how they behave? Write down three things. Assumption one: who your customers are. Assumption two: what problem they have. Assumption three: how urgently they want a solution.

Be honest. Write down what you actually believe, not what you think sounds good. "I believe my customers are small business owners who want to save time on accounting" is a real assumption. "My customers need my product" is not — it is a hope.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 3 — "Market Assumptions"]

[ON CAMERA]

Category two: market assumptions. What do you believe about the size and nature of the market? Assumption four: how many potential customers exist. Assumption five: how customers currently solve the problem you want to address.

That second one is especially important. If your customers already have a solution — even an imperfect one — you need to understand what it is and why yours would be better. If they do not currently solve the problem at all, you need to ask whether that is because the problem is not painful enough to motivate action.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 4 — "Solution Assumptions"]

[ON CAMERA]

Category three: solution assumptions. What do you believe about your product or service itself? Assumption six: why your solution is better than alternatives. Assumption seven: what customers will pay. Assumption eight: your delivery or logistics assumption — can you actually get the product to the customer the way you imagine?

Pay special attention to assumption seven — pricing. Most first-time entrepreneurs either have no idea what to charge or they pick a number that "feels right" without calculating whether that number covers their costs and leaves a margin. We will go deeper on pricing later in the course, but for now, write down what you believe.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 5 — "Risk Assessment"]

[ON CAMERA]

Now comes the most important part. Look at all eight assumptions you have written down. Which one, if it turned out to be wrong, would most damage your business?

Copy that assumption into the "riskiest assumption" box. Then answer two questions about it. First: what evidence do you currently have that it is true? Be honest — anecdotes from friends do not count as market evidence. And second: how could you test this assumption this week with minimal cost?

[PAUSE]

That second question leads directly to your real-world activity.

[SCREEN SHARE: Real-World Activity — "The Customer Discovery Conversation"]

[ON CAMERA]

Your assignment this week is called the Customer Discovery Conversation. Here is what I want you to do.

Identify one person who represents your target customer. Not your friend who will tell you what you want to hear. An actual person who fits the description you wrote in the worksheet.

Have a real conversation with them. But here is the crucial part — you are not pitching your idea. You are listening. You are trying to understand their problem.

Ask them these questions:

[GRAPHIC: Four questions appearing:
1. "What is the hardest part of [the problem you think you're solving]?"
2. "How do you handle it today?"
3. "What have you already tried?"
4. "If you could change one thing about how you deal with this, what would it be?"]

[ON CAMERA]

Do not mention your solution until the very end, and only to ask if it sounds useful. Remember the Mom Test — ask about their life, not about your idea.

After the conversation, write down the one thing that surprised you most about what they said. That surprise is data. It is telling you something your assumptions did not account for.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Look, I know this feels uncomfortable. Talking to strangers about their problems is not something most of us do naturally. But I promise you — this one conversation will teach you more about your business idea than a week of planning at your desk. This is what "get out of the building" means in practice.

[PAUSE]

So here is your action list for this week:

[GRAPHIC: Action items:
1. Write down your business idea in one clear sentence
2. List every assumption your idea rests on (aim for at least 8)
3. Rank your assumptions by risk — which one, if wrong, would kill the business?
4. Schedule at least one conversation with a potential customer this week]

[ON CAMERA]

Get the worksheet done. Get the conversation scheduled. And come back tomorrow for our weekly wrap-up.

You are building something real. This is the first step.
