## Day 1: One Page, Nine Questions

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Week Four and to Module Two — "Shape Your Business Model." This is where your opportunity becomes a system.

Over the next three weeks, you are going to take the business idea you chose in Module One and turn it into something far more concrete. And it starts today, with one of the most powerful tools in modern entrepreneurship: the Business Model Canvas.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Let me ask you something. If someone asked you right now to describe your business, what would you say?

Most entrepreneurs talk about their product. "I make bread." "I give math lessons." "I drive people from one place to another." But a product is not a business. A business is a system — a set of interlocking choices about customers, value, channels, relationships, revenue, resources, activities, partners, and costs.

The Business Model Canvas, developed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, puts all nine of those choices on a single page so you can see the whole system at once.

[GRAPHIC: A large, clean Business Model Canvas template showing all nine blocks labeled:
Left side: Key Partners | Key Activities | Key Resources
Center: Value Propositions
Right side: Customer Relationships | Channels | Customer Segments
Bottom left: Cost Structure
Bottom right: Revenue Streams]

[ON CAMERA]

Nine blocks. Nine questions. One page. That is your entire business.

Let me show you how it works with something familiar.

Imagine a bakery in Bishkek. The baker might think his business is about making excellent bread. But a business model thinker sees it differently.

[GRAPHIC: Bakery example canvas being filled in block by block as the presenter speaks]

[ON CAMERA]

Who are the customers — local families, hotels, or both? What do they value — freshness, price, or variety? How does the bread reach them — do they come to the shop, or does someone deliver? What is the relationship — do they know the baker by name, or is it purely transactional? How does money flow — cash each morning, or bulk orders from restaurants?

Those are five questions, and we have not even touched the kitchen yet.

Then there are four more about how the operation works. What physical and human resources are required — an oven, flour suppliers, a delivery person? What activities must happen every day — baking, cleaning, ordering, marketing? Who are the essential partners — the flour mill, the gas company, the landlord? And when you add up everything it costs to run this system, does the money coming in exceed the money going out?

Only when you can answer all nine questions do you have a business — not just a job.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you two more quick examples to show how this works across very different businesses.

[GRAPHIC: Tutoring business canvas sketch]

[ON CAMERA]

Consider a tutoring business. At the center is the value proposition: students pass their exams and parents feel confident their children are learning. On the right side, the customer segment is middle-school students whose parents are anxious about university entrance exams. The channel is neighbor-to-neighbor word of mouth. The customer relationship is personal — the tutor knows each student's weaknesses. Revenue is a weekly fee paid in cash.

On the left side, the key resource is the tutor's knowledge and reputation. The key activity is preparing and delivering lessons. The key partner might be a school that refers struggling students. The cost structure is simple: the tutor's time and perhaps printed worksheets.

[GRAPHIC: Taxi driver canvas sketch]

[ON CAMERA]

Now consider a local taxi driver. Value proposition: reliable transport on demand. Customer segment: people without cars who need to reach the market, hospital, or airport. Channels: phone calls and standing at a regular corner. Customer relationship: friendly and local — passengers know his face. Revenue: a fare per trip.

Key resource: the car. Key activities: driving, maintaining the vehicle, being available. Key partner: a mechanic who fixes the car quickly. Costs: fuel, maintenance, his own time.

[PAUSE]

Two very different businesses. Same nine questions. Same canvas.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Now, here is the most important thing I can tell you about the canvas: the power is not that it produces perfect answers on the first attempt. It is that it forces you to confront the questions you have been avoiding.

Most first-time entrepreneurs have strong ideas in two or three blocks and vague or empty thinking in the others. That is completely normal. The point of filling it in today — even imperfectly — is to see the gaps.

An empty block is not a failure. It is a signal that you have work to do before you invest real resources into this idea.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "Use sticky notes when filling in your canvas for the first time. Each block gets its own color. This way you can move and remove ideas without starting over. Think of it as a thinking tool, not a finished document."]

[ON CAMERA]

Keep your first canvas in pencil, not pen. The canvas is a living document. As you learn more about your customers, your costs, and your market, you will change it. Successful companies revise their business model canvas many times as they grow. What matters is not getting it perfect on day one, but developing the discipline to think about your business as a complete system — not just a single product or service.

[PAUSE]

Questions to think about before tomorrow. Which of the nine blocks do you feel most confident about right now? Which one feels the most unclear or uncertain? And here is a challenging one: if you removed one block entirely, would the business still work? If the answer is yes, that block might not be essential to your model. If the answer is no, that block is critical — and you had better understand it deeply.

Tomorrow we dive into the right side of the canvas — the customer-facing half. Who you serve, what you offer, how you reach them, and how you make money.

## Day 2: The Right Side — Who Are Your Customers and Why Do They Care?

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome back. Yesterday we saw the full Business Model Canvas — all nine blocks on one page. Today we are going deep on the right side — the customer-facing half. This is where value is created and captured in the marketplace.

Let us start with the first block on the right side: Customer Segments.

[PAUSE]

Who are you serving? This sounds like a simple question. It is not. Answering "everyone" is not an answer. Every successful business serves a specific group of people whose needs, habits, and economic situation are distinct enough to require a tailored approach. The sharper your definition of your customer segment, the better every other decision on the right side of the canvas becomes.

[GRAPHIC: Types of customer segments:
- MASS MARKET — broad population, similar needs (phone company, flour mill)
- NICHE MARKET — very specific group, particular needs (hiking equipment for women, meals for diabetes patients)
- SEGMENTED — two or more distinct groups (bank serving individuals AND small businesses)
- MULTI-SIDED PLATFORM — two different groups that need each other (newspaper: readers + advertisers)]

[ON CAMERA]

For most small businesses in Central Asia, you are choosing between mass market and niche. And niche is usually a better starting point — because you can serve those customers exceptionally well with limited resources.

[PAUSE]

Once you know who your customer is, the next question is your Value Proposition. Why should they choose you over any alternative — including doing nothing?

A value proposition is not a description of your product. It is a statement of the benefit your customer receives.

The baker does not sell bread. She sells the comfort of fresh bread on the table every morning. The tutor does not sell lessons. He sells the relief of a parent who no longer worries about their child's future.

Ask yourself: what problem does my customer have, and how does my business solve it better than any other option?

[GRAPHIC: How value propositions win:
- NEWNESS — something that never existed in this market
- PERFORMANCE — same thing, done significantly better
- CUSTOMIZATION — tailored to individual needs
- PRICE — same value, cheaper
- CONVENIENCE/ACCESSIBILITY — making available what was hard to get]

[ON CAMERA]

Most small businesses in early markets win on a combination of convenience, personal relationship, and price. Understanding which mechanism drives your value is essential to how you communicate and market your business.

[PAUSE]

Channels are how your value proposition reaches your customer. There are two main types: owned channels — your own shop, your delivery person, your social media page — and partner channels — a distributor, a retailer who stocks your product, a referral network.

Most small businesses start with direct, owned channels because they are cheaper and give you more control and more customer feedback. The bakery starts by selling from her home. The tutor starts by knocking on neighbors' doors. As the business grows, channels expand — a market stall, then a delivery route, then a partner cafe that stocks her goods.

[PAUSE]

Customer Relationships describes how you interact with your customers throughout the relationship. This ranges from highly personal — the baker who knows every customer's name and remembers their preferences — to completely self-service.

[B-ROLL: A vendor at a market greeting regular customers by name, remembering their usual order]

[ON CAMERA]

In Central Asian culture, where trust and personal connection drive commerce, many businesses thrive on deeply personal relationships that feel more like friendship than transaction. This is a competitive advantage worth understanding and protecting. It is harder to scale than self-service, but it generates loyalty that competitors cannot easily replicate.

[PAUSE]

Revenue Streams is where many first-time entrepreneurs go vague. "I will charge for my product" is not enough.

[GRAPHIC: Revenue questions to answer:
- How MUCH will you charge?
- How will customers PAY — cash, transfer, credit, subscription?
- FIXED price or negotiated?
- VOLUME discounts?
- ONE-TIME revenue or RECURRING?
- Multiple revenue streams?]

[ON CAMERA]

Some businesses earn revenue from multiple streams. A tutoring center might charge per student but also charge schools for access to their curriculum. Understanding your revenue logic determines whether the right side of your canvas generates enough income to pay for the left side.

[PAUSE]

Let me put it all together with an example.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "A woman in Osh starts a home-cooked lunch delivery service. Customer segment: office workers who cannot leave their desk at midday. Value proposition: warm, home-style food — the kind their mothers would make — delivered to their desk by noon. Channel: a Telegram group where orders are placed by 9am. Customer relationship: warm and personal — she remembers who is vegetarian, who avoids spicy food. Revenue: daily cash payment per meal. Every block on the right side tells the same story."]

[ON CAMERA]

That is the key. The right side of the canvas must tell a coherent story. Your customer segment has a problem. Your value proposition solves it. Your channels make it easy to access. Your customer relationship maintains trust. And your revenue streams capture enough value to sustain the business.

When all five elements align and reinforce each other, you have a business model that can survive in the real market. When they contradict or ignore each other, you have a product waiting to fail.

[PAUSE]

Questions for today. Is your customer segment narrow enough that you could describe your ideal customer in one specific sentence? What would your customer do instead of buying from you, and why is your solution better? And is your revenue model simple enough for a customer to understand in ten seconds?

Tomorrow, we flip to the other side — operations, resources, partnerships, and costs. The part that determines whether you can actually deliver what you are promising.

## Day 3: The Left Side — How Will You Make It Happen?

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Day Three. Yesterday we mapped the right side of the canvas — who you serve and how you create value. Today we tackle the left side — how you actually deliver that value. This is where dreams meet logistics.

[PAUSE]

If the right side of the Business Model Canvas is about market and value, the left side is about execution. It answers the question every investor and every creditor eventually asks: do you actually have the capacity to deliver what you are promising?

Many business plans sound beautiful on paper but collapse because the entrepreneur never seriously thought through whether they have the resources, capabilities, and relationships to make it work in the real world.

Let us go through each block.

[PAUSE]

Key Resources are the most important assets required to make your business model function. These fall into four categories.

[GRAPHIC: Four types of Key Resources:
PHYSICAL — machinery, buildings, vehicles, inventory
INTELLECTUAL — brand, proprietary knowledge, customer databases
HUMAN — skilled people (master tailor, licensed pharmacist, knowledgeable driver)
FINANCIAL — cash, credit lines, investment capital]

[ON CAMERA]

Different business models require different mixes. A manufacturing business is resource-heavy on the physical side. A consulting business is resource-heavy on the human side. A software company is resource-heavy on the intellectual side.

Knowing what your business truly runs on helps you protect those resources and avoid underinvesting in the thing your whole model depends upon.

[PAUSE]

Key Activities are the most critical things your business must do to operate. These also fall into categories.

Production activities involve making or delivering your product — baking bread, cleaning carpets, driving cargo. Problem-solving activities involve expertise applied to unique customer problems — accounting, legal advice, medical diagnosis. Platform activities involve managing connections between parties.

Most small businesses are primarily production-focused. The key is to identify which activities are central to your value proposition and which are peripheral. If you are a bakery, baking is central. Bookkeeping is not. Understanding this distinction helps you prioritize your time and decide what to delegate or outsource.

[PAUSE]

Key Partnerships are the network of suppliers and partners that make your model work.

[GRAPHIC: Types of partnerships:
- BUYER-SUPPLIER — basic supply chain (baker ↔ flour supplier)
- STRATEGIC ALLIANCES — non-competing businesses that benefit both (bakery ↔ coffee shop referrals)
- JOINT VENTURES — shared risk, cost, and reward for a common goal
- CO-OPETITION — competitors who collaborate in some areas (taxi drivers sharing dispatch)]

[ON CAMERA]

The reasons businesses build partnerships fall into patterns too. Some partnerships exist to optimize costs — buying from a wholesaler rather than manufacturing inputs yourself. Some exist to reduce risk — sharing the cost of expensive equipment. Some exist to acquire capabilities you do not have — a bakery partnering with a delivery service because she cannot afford her own vehicle. And some partnerships open doors — a local business partnering with an international organization to access training, certification, or markets it could not reach alone.

[PAUSE]

Cost Structure is the final block, and in many ways the most sobering.

Every decision on the left side of your canvas has a cost. Every resource you require, every activity you perform, every partnership you build — it all costs something.

[GRAPHIC: Two types of costs:
FIXED COSTS — stay the same regardless of output (rent, loan repayments, salaries)
VARIABLE COSTS — change with output (ingredients per cake, fuel per delivery, commission per sale)]

[ON CAMERA]

Understanding your cost structure helps you determine your break-even point — the minimum volume of sales you need before the business stops losing money.

Some business models are cost-driven — built to minimize costs so they can offer the lowest possible price. A market stall selling basic produce is cost-driven. Other models are value-driven — built to prioritize quality and service, charging a premium to cover higher costs. A high-end catering company is value-driven. Most small businesses start cost-driven and gradually develop value-driven elements as they build reputation.

[PAUSE]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Now, here is the critical connection. The left side must connect to the right side. Every key resource you identify should be necessary to deliver your value proposition. Every key activity should be required to serve your customer segments. Every key partnership should help you reach your channels or strengthen your offering.

And when you look at your cost structure alongside your revenue streams, the mathematics must eventually work in your favor. Revenue must exceed costs for the business to survive.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "The most common mistake on the left side of the canvas is listing resources and activities that sound impressive but are not actually necessary. Every resource costs money and every activity costs time. If a block does not directly support your value proposition or your ability to reach customers, question whether it belongs in your model at all."]

[ON CAMERA]

Many first-time entrepreneurs are shocked to discover, when they complete the canvas honestly, that their costs are far higher or their revenue far lower than they assumed. This is not a reason to give up. It is a reason to redesign your model before you invest real money.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Before we close today, I want to share a deeper perspective that connects to everything we have discussed.

There is a long tradition in many cultures, including in Central Asia, of thinking about business in terms of relationships rather than systems. A business is your family's livelihood, your neighbor's employer, your community's bread. This relational way of thinking is not wrong. It is a profound strength. But it can cause entrepreneurs to neglect systems thinking — which is different from but not opposed to relational thinking.

[PAUSE]

Thinking systemically means asking: if I stepped away for a month, would this business still function?

If the answer is no — if everything depends on your personal presence, energy, and relationships — then you have a job, not a business. A system is something that can operate, at least partially, without you.

Building systems does not mean removing the human touch. It means documenting processes, training people, building supplier relationships that survive on paper, and creating channels that work even when you are not watching.

The Business Model Canvas is one of the most practical tools for beginning to see your idea as a system. It is also an act of stewardship — taking seriously the responsibility to build something sustainable, not just something that works today.

[PAUSE]

Questions for today. What is the single most important resource your business cannot operate without? Which of your key activities could you outsource to a partner without losing quality? And if you list all your monthly costs honestly, do your projected revenues cover them?

And two deeper questions to sit with: is your current business idea a system, or does it depend entirely on you being present at all times? What is one step you could take this week to make your business slightly less dependent on your personal daily involvement?

Tomorrow, Aijan fills in her canvas — and discovers that confidence and clarity are not the same thing.

[ANIMATION: A split-screen moment. On one side, the word "HOBBY" with scattered, informal icons — a heart, a kitchen, friends applauding. For the first time, Aijan understood the difference between a hobby and a business. A hobby runs on passion and opportunity. A business runs on systems.

The canvas had not discouraged her. It had clarified exactly what she needed to build before she opened her doors.

"Now you are building a business," he said. "Before, you were just imagining one."

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

The canvas did not stop Aijan's dream. It showed her the path. And notice — the gaps were not failures. They were directions. Every empty box told her exactly what she needed to learn, build, or secure next.

That is the power of seeing your whole business on one page.

## Day 5: Action Steps — Building Your Business Model Canvas

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day Five — action day. This week you learned the Business Model Canvas, and today you are going to build yours.

I am going to walk you through the worksheet block by block, and then I will explain your real-world activity. By the end of this week, you should have a complete first draft of your canvas — with honest assessments of where you are strong and where you need more work.

[SCREEN SHARE: Business Model Canvas worksheet — Section 1: Customer Segments]

[ON CAMERA]

Let us start on the right side, because your customer determines everything else.

Customer Segments. Describe your primary customer group in specific terms. Who exactly are they? Age, location, situation, income level? Avoid "everyone." The more specific you are, the better.

If you have a secondary customer segment — a second distinct group you will serve with different needs — describe them separately. But many early-stage businesses should focus on one segment first.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 2 — Value Propositions]

[ON CAMERA]

Value Propositions. What problem do you solve and how? Remember — this is not a description of your product. It is a statement of the benefit your customer receives.

Then: what makes you different from alternatives? Be honest. "Better quality" is not specific enough. Describe the concrete difference a customer would experience. Would they save time? Get something they cannot get elsewhere? Feel more confident about an important event?

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 3 — Channels]

[ON CAMERA]

Channels. This has three parts. How customers learn about you — how will someone who has never heard of you discover that you exist? How customers buy from you — what is the easiest, most natural way to make a purchase? And how you deliver — how does the product or service actually reach the customer?

If any of these three are unclear, that is a gap in your model that needs addressing.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 4 — Customer Relationships]

[ON CAMERA]

Customer Relationships. What type of relationship do your customers expect? Personal, self-service, community, automated? What level of personal attention can you realistically offer?

And critically: how do you keep customers coming back? Loyalty discount? Consistent quality? Community? Regular check-ins? Retention is almost always cheaper than acquisition.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 5 — Revenue Streams]

[ON CAMERA]

Revenue Streams. How do you earn revenue? Per-unit sale, subscription, service fee, commission? List every way money comes into the business. Do not assume there is only one.

And your pricing approach — fixed, negotiated, volume discount, market rate? How do you decide what to charge? If the answer is "I picked a number that felt right," go back and calculate your actual costs first.

[SCREEN SHARE: Sections 6-8 — Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships]

[ON CAMERA]

Now the left side. Key Resources — physical, human, intellectual. What do you own versus what you need to acquire? Key Activities — what must happen every day for the business to function? What is central to your value proposition and what is secondary but necessary? And Key Partnerships — who provides your essential inputs? Do you have confirmed relationships or is this still an assumption?

Be especially honest about partnerships. An assumed partnership is not a partnership. It is a hope. Mark clearly which partnerships are confirmed and which need to be established.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 9 — Cost Structure]

[ON CAMERA]

Cost Structure. List your fixed costs — monthly expenses that stay the same regardless of sales. Then your variable costs — expenses that increase as you produce more. And identify your single largest cost.

When you have both your revenue streams and your cost structure filled in, do the math. Does the revenue cover the costs? If not, something in the model needs to change — and the canvas just told you what to redesign.

[PAUSE]
