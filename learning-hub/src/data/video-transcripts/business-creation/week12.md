## Day 1: The One-Page Business Plan

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Week 12. The final week of this course.

[PAUSE]

Let that sit for a moment. Twelve weeks ago, you walked into this course with an idea — maybe a clear one, maybe a vague one — and a willingness to learn. Since then, you have discovered real problems through customer research. You have defined your customer with specificity. You have designed a business model that makes financial sense. You have validated your assumptions with real data. You have designed an MVP. And you have started finding actual paying customers.

That is not a small thing. That is extraordinary.

[ON CAMERA]

This week, everything you have built comes together. You are going to synthesize twelve weeks of work into a single document — your one-page business plan. You are going to build a 90-day launch calendar that bridges the gap between this classroom and the real world. And on Day 5, you are going to deliver your final business pitch.

Let us start with the plan itself.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Most people associate "business plan" with a thick document filled with market research, financial projections, and competitive analysis. It takes months to write and is read once before being filed away.

That kind of document served a purpose in a world where businesses needed to raise capital from banks who required formal documentation. But for the vast majority of small businesses in Central Asian markets — businesses that grow through customers, not investors — a fifty-page business plan is not a useful tool.

It is a form of productive procrastination that delays the moment of actually launching.

[ON CAMERA]

A one-page business plan does something different. It forces you to express your business with the clarity and precision that proves you actually understand it.

[GRAPHIC: "One page = synthesis, not simplification"]

Fitting your entire business concept on a single page is not a simplification. It is an act of synthesis. Every word must earn its place. Every section must be genuinely essential. The discipline of creating it forces you to resolve ambiguities, confront gaps, and make decisions you have been avoiding.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I have found: entrepreneurs who cannot complete a one-page business plan usually discover, through the process of trying, that they have not actually resolved a key question about their business. The plan reveals the gap. And that is valuable.

[ON CAMERA]

The one-page plan connects directly to everything you have built over eleven weeks. Nothing on this plan should be a new idea.

Your problem statement connects to your customer discovery research from Weeks 1 through 3. Your customer definition connects to your segmentation and persona work. Your solution and value proposition connect to the VPC and the refinements you made in Week 10. Your revenue model connects to your business model canvas from Weeks 4 through 6. Your competitive advantage connects to your validation findings from Weeks 7 through 9. Your financial summary connects to your cost structure and revenue modeling. Your MVP description connects to Week 10. Your traction strategy connects to Week 11.

It is all work you have already done, now expressed with maximum clarity.

[GRAPHIC: One-page plan template with eight sections listed]

The one-page plan has eight essential sections. Let me walk through each one.

[ON CAMERA]

Business name and mission — one sentence that captures what you do and why. One sentence. If you need more than one sentence, your mission is not yet clear.

The problem — a crisp statement of the specific pain you are addressing for a specific group of people. Use the language your customers used in interviews, not the language you would use in a textbook.

The solution — what you offer that addresses that pain. Focus on outcomes, not product specifications.

The customer — who specifically you serve. A clear segment, not a vague generality. Not "anyone who wants cakes" but "families in central Bishkek celebrating birthdays and milestones."

The revenue model — how you make money. One or two sentences. What you charge, when customers pay, and how.

The competitive advantage — the specific reason customers should choose you over alternatives. And let me say this clearly: be honest here.

[SERIOUS TONE]

If your competitive advantage is simply that you reliably show up in a market where competitors are unreliable — write that. Reliability is a real competitive advantage. It is a more honest one than claiming to have superior technology or proprietary knowledge you do not actually have.

[ON CAMERA]

The financial summary — your monthly cost to operate, your price per unit, and your break-even volume. Three numbers that tell anyone whether your business makes mathematical sense.

And the MVP — what you are launching first. One precise paragraph that matches your Week 10 MVP definition statement.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

One temptation when completing the one-page plan is to write aspirationally — to describe the business you wish you were building rather than the business you are actually building. Resist that temptation. Write what is true.

[ON CAMERA]

When you complete your one-page plan, here is the test. Share it with someone who knows nothing about your business. Ask them to read it and then explain back to you what your business does, who it serves, and why someone would choose it.

If their explanation matches your intention, your plan is clear. If it does not, revise until it does. Clarity on paper is a proxy for clarity in the founder's mind.

[GRAPHIC: "Write it in language you would use in a conversation. If you wouldn't say it to your grandmother, don't write it in your plan."]

Use plain language. If you would not say "we leverage a differentiated value proposition to capture market share in an underserved segment" to your grandmother, do not write it in your plan. Write instead: "We are the only baker in our neighborhood who shows up on time. Customers know they can count on us for celebrations that matter."

[ON CAMERA]

Your assignment: complete your one-page business plan using the worksheet template. Every section should draw directly from work you have done in previous weeks. No new inventions. Just clarity.

## Day 2: The 90-Day Launch Calendar

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Yesterday you completed your one-page business plan — a clear, honest document that captures everything your business is and intends to be. Today we build the bridge between that document and real life.

Because here is the truth that nobody tells you.

[PAUSE]

The space between completing a business course and running a real business is where most entrepreneurial dreams die. Not because people are lazy or uncommitted. But because the transition from learning to doing is unclear.

What exactly do you do on the Monday after this course ends? What do you do in week two? Month two?

Without a concrete action plan, the urgent always crowds out the important. Family obligations, financial pressures, everyday life — they fill the space where business-building should happen.

The 90-day launch calendar is your bridge from student to entrepreneur.

[GRAPHIC: "90-Day Launch Calendar" with three phases: Foundation (1-30), Traction (31-60), Optimization (61-90)]

[ON CAMERA]

The first thirty days are your foundation phase. In this phase, you are building the structures that allow your business to operate.

Set up your Telegram channel or Instagram profile if you have not already. Tell your personal network that you have launched. Complete your first three to five orders. Establish your operational rhythm — the daily and weekly routines that keep the business running.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Most importantly, gather feedback from your first customers with the same discipline you brought to your earlier customer interviews. What did they love? What confused them? What would make them recommend you immediately to a friend?

[ON CAMERA]

Your 30-day milestone should be specific and modest. Specific means a number. Not "get some customers" but "complete five paid orders." And modest means achievable — something you can hit even if some things go wrong.

Here is why modest matters. Entrepreneurs who set overly ambitious early milestones and miss them often lose confidence and give up, when in reality their business was progressing normally. A modest milestone you hit is more valuable — psychologically and informationally — than an ambitious one you miss.

[GRAPHIC: "30-day milestone example: 5 paid orders, first income generated, operational process established"]

[ON CAMERA]

Days 31 through 60 are your traction phase. You have proven your MVP works in a basic sense — real customers have paid you real money. Now you systematically test your primary traction channel with greater discipline, work toward your first repeat customers, and begin to understand your funnel numbers.

In this phase you should also complete your first honest financial review. What did you actually earn? What did you actually spend? What is your real profit margin — not the theoretical one from your business model canvas?

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Many entrepreneurs discover in this phase that their costs were higher than expected or their conversion rates were lower. This is not failure. This is the discovery that makes your business stronger. You cannot optimize what you do not measure.

[ON CAMERA]

Your 60-day milestone should represent genuine progress. If your 30-day milestone was five customers, your 60-day milestone might be twelve customers with at least two repeat purchases and a positive referral rate. The metrics you choose should reflect what you are learning about your specific business.

[GRAPHIC: "60-day milestone example: 12 paid orders, 2 repeat customers, primary channel identified and optimized"]

Days 61 through 90 are your optimization phase. By now you have sixty days of real operating data. You know which customers buy most reliably. You know which traction channel produces the best results. You know what objections come up most often. You know where your operations are most fragile.

Optimization means systematically addressing each of these findings. Improving the weakest part of your funnel. Doubling down on your best channel. Creating systems that allow your business to run more smoothly without requiring heroic effort from you every single day.

[ON CAMERA]

Your 90-day milestone is your proof of concept. Not proof that someone liked your idea — you proved that back in Week 8. Proof that someone will pay you, repeatedly, and tell others to do the same.

[GRAPHIC: "90-day milestone example: 25 paid orders, 5+ repeat customers, monthly revenue covering all costs with profit, 1-2 unprompted referral customers"]

For many Central Asian small businesses, a strong 90-day milestone looks like: twenty to thirty active customers. Consistent monthly revenue that covers your costs with meaningful profit. At least five repeat customers. And one or two unprompted referrals.

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

If you hit those numbers, you have a business. You may have a small business. But you have a real one. And from a real one, you can grow.

[ON CAMERA]

One important element of your 90-day calendar is weekly tracking. Do not wait for milestone reviews to discover that things have been going wrong for weeks.

The simplest system: every Sunday evening, write down three numbers. Orders completed this week. Revenue earned this week. New customers acquired this week. Review those numbers against last week and against your milestone trajectory. If the trend is positive, continue. If it is flat or declining, investigate before it becomes a crisis.

[PAUSE]

Let me also address the first 72 hours. Because those first three days after this course ends are the most dangerous and the most important.

In the first 72 hours, you have maximum motivation and maximum clarity. Both will fade. Not because you are weak — because that is how human psychology works. The energy of completion dissipates. The daily grind resumes. Your business plan, which felt urgent on Friday, feels daunting on Monday.

[ON CAMERA]

So decide right now: what specific action will you take in the first 72 hours after this course? Not an intention. An action. With a date, a time, and a person attached to it.

"On Monday at 9 AM, I will post my first offer in the professional women's Telegram group."

"On Tuesday, I will bake three sample cakes and photograph them."

"On Wednesday, I will call my first five potential customers and tell them I have launched."

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Write it down. The specificity is what makes it real.

Your assignment: build your complete 90-day calendar using the worksheet. At least three specific, measurable milestones per phase. A weekly tracking system. And your first 72-hour action plan.

## Day 4: Aijan's Business Plan and the Gift of Teaching

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Story

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

On the last day of the course, Aijan arrived early. She had been awake since five in the morning — not from anxiety, but from something that felt like clarity.

[PAUSE]

She had been thinking about the journey from Week 1 to Week 12, and she kept coming back to a single image: the notebook from her first day, where she had written "I want to bake cakes" with a question mark at the end.

Now, twelve weeks later, the question mark was gone.

[ANIMATION: Notebook page from Week 1 — "I want to bake cakes?" in uncertain handwriting. Dissolve to a printed one-page business plan, clean and precise.]

Her one-page business plan fit on a single printed page and said exactly what needed to be said.

Business name: Aijan's Celebration Cakes. Mission: To be the most reliable celebration baker in our neighborhood. Problem: People in Bishkek cannot count on home-based cake vendors to show up on time for important events. Solution: Custom celebration cakes — three proven flavors — ordered via Telegram with a three-day minimum, delivered within five kilometers, on time, every time.

Customer: Families in central Bishkek celebrating birthdays, graduations, and milestones. Revenue model: Per-cake pricing based on size and flavor, cash on delivery. Competitive advantage: Reliability.

And the MVP? She had already launched it. She had twelve customers.

[ON CAMERA]

Twelve customers. The number had seemed impossibly large when she wrote her first goals in Week 2. Now it felt like the beginning.

She had one customer who had ordered three times. Two who had referred a friend. She had completed thirty-seven cakes in eight weeks of operating. And she had not once been late. Not once delivered something that was not exactly what she promised.

That record was the most important line in her entire business plan, even though it did not appear on the page.

[ANIMATION: The Four Hats session. Aijan standing in front of a small group, her one-page plan projected or pinned to a board. She speaks calmly and clearly, pointing to specific sections.]

The Four Hats session was the hardest part of the day. When it was her turn to present, she stood up and told the story clearly. The problem she found through interviews. The VPC she had revised twice. The MoSCoW features she had cut. The Telegram strategy that had outperformed Instagram three to one. The repeat customers who made her believe the model was working.

Then the hats came out.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

The Black Hat question was the sharpest. "What is the single biggest risk that could kill your business in the first ninety days?"

She had thought about this.

"My reputation," she said. "If I miss one important delivery — if someone's birthday cake does not show up — word travels faster through Telegram groups than good news does. One failure undoes ten successes in this market. So my biggest risk is also my biggest opportunity: reliability."

The group was quiet for a moment. Then her facilitator nodded slowly.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

After the session, her friend Mira waited outside. Mira had attended one of the early course evenings as a visitor and had been thinking since then about the tailoring business she had always imagined.

[ANIMATION: Aijan and Mira walking through a Bishkek neighborhood. Talking animatedly. Aijan gesturing, explaining concepts. Mira listening, occasionally stopping to write something in a small notebook.]

"Tell me everything," Mira said. And Aijan laughed.

They walked for two hours through the neighborhood while Aijan explained the VPC, the customer interview process, the MoSCoW framework. She realized, with some surprise, that she understood the frameworks better now that she was explaining them than she had when she first learned them.

[ON CAMERA]

That evening, Aijan added a new entry to her notebook. Not about cakes. About what happened when you understand something well enough to teach it.

[ANIMATION: Notebook close-up. Aijan's handwriting: "Mira's first customer interview is scheduled for next week. I said I would come along." Below that: Mira's name and the date, written the way Aijan once wrote her own name at the top of a course registration form.]

She had spent twelve weeks becoming an entrepreneur. In the conversation with Mira, she had spent two hours becoming something else — a person who could multiply what she had learned.

[ON CAMERA]

The question mark in her first notebook was gone. But Mira's notebook, she imagined, was probably blank right now — waiting for the same uncertain beginning that every business requires.

The thought filled her with something she recognized as purpose. Not just making beautiful cakes on time. But knowing that the thing she had learned could travel further than her five-kilometer delivery radius.

One person at a time.

## KYRGYZ VERSION - Story: Аижандын ашканасы (Aijan's Kitchen)

**Video Length:** ~12 minutes | **Type:** Story (Central Asia)

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

This is the final chapter of Aijan's story. And it is the one I have been waiting to tell you.

[PAUSE]

On the last evening of the course, Aijan arrived at the community center with a tray of manty she had made that morning — enough for the whole group and the facilitators. She set it on the table at the back without ceremony and sat down.

Six months had passed since the evening she arrived with a notebook and a vague plan to deliver food to offices. Everything in that plan had changed except the food itself.

[ANIMATION: Community center room. A tray of manty on a back table, steaming slightly. People filing in, greeting each other. Aijan sitting calmly, notebooks stacked beside her.]

[ON CAMERA]

Aijan's Kitchen now served forty lunches a day to eight office buildings in the central Bishkek area. She had three people helping her.

[ANIMATION: Kitchen scene — a bustling morning. Aijan and her aunt Gulnara working side by side, steam rising from large pots. A young man with an electric scooter helmet — Daniil — loading insulated bags. A part-time helper organizing containers.]

Her aunt Gulnara, who had needed work after her husband left for seasonal labor in Moscow. Gulnara had come in after a long conversation over tea in Aijan's apartment kitchen — one of those conversations where you realize family can also be business partners, if the terms are clear.

A delivery driver named Daniil, a young man with a small electric scooter who had answered her Telegram job post.

And a part-time helper on Tuesdays and Thursdays when orders were heaviest. Temirlan was now in his second year of economics and had handed off delivery to Daniil, though he still helped on weekends sometimes.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Let me tell you about the hardest week. The week she brought Gulnara in.

They had argued about recipes on the third day. Gulnara wanted to add beshbarmak to the menu because it was the most important Kyrgyz dish, and she felt its absence said something incomplete about the business. Aijan had explained, with the MoSCoW framework in her head, that beshbarmak required customers to slow down, eat with their hands, and could not be eaten at a desk during a twenty-minute lunch break.

[ANIMATION: Kitchen scene. Aijan and Gulnara facing each other across the prep table. Not hostile, but firm. Both making their case.]

Gulnara said that maybe the customers needed to slow down.

Aijan said that was not a business decision. It was a philosophy.

They reached a compromise. Beshbarmak on Fridays, by pre-order only, at a higher price point, for customers who wanted to take it home after work.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

It had become one of the most popular Friday items.

That is what it looks like when a framework meets real life. MoSCoW did not tell Aijan to refuse the beshbarmak. It helped her think clearly about how to include it without compromising the business model. Must Have — no. Could Have — maybe. Friday pre-order special — yes.

[ANIMATION: Simple graphic — revenue numbers appearing on screen. Monthly revenue: 180,000 som. Monthly profit: 55,000 som. Previous accounting salary: 18,000 som.]

Monthly revenue: 180,000 som. Monthly profit after all costs, wages, and ingredients: 55,000 som.

Her accounting salary had been 18,000 som. She had left the job eight weeks after launching the MVP.

[ON CAMERA]

Her one-page business plan said: "Aijan's Kitchen delivers freshly cooked Kyrgyz office lunches to central Bishkek. We are the team that shows up every day, on time, with food that tastes like it was made this morning, because it was. We serve workers who are tired of reheated ashkana food and want something that actually nourishes them. We charge 200 to 220 som per portion. We break even at 82 portions per day. We serve 40. We are growing."

[PAUSE]

[ON CAMERA]

The Four Hats session was the hardest part. When the Black Hat came around, someone asked: "What is the single thing most likely to damage this business in the next ninety days?"

Aijan had thought about this.

"My reputation inside specific Telegram groups," she said. "In this city, a complaint shared in the right group reaches a thousand people by morning. One bad delivery — food that is cold, an order that is wrong, a customer who feels dismissed — can undo three months of good deliveries. The advantage and the risk are exactly the same thing: how fast information travels."

[ON CAMERA]

After the session, her mother called from Tokmok. Not on a schedule. Just at the right moment, the way her mother always seemed to call.

[ANIMATION: Aijan stepping outside the community center. Cold evening air — her breath visible. Phone to her ear. The lights of Bishkek in the background.]

Aijan stood in the cold air of the Bishkek evening while her mother asked how it had gone. Aijan told her the numbers: forty lunches a day. Three employees. 55,000 som profit last month.

Her mother was quiet for a moment. Then she said:

"Your grandmother would have understood this. She fed half the village for thirty years from a kitchen smaller than yours. She always said: cook it right, give enough, and they will come back."

[PAUSE]

Then her mother paused.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

"Your father called me yesterday."

Aijan waited.

"He said he went to see your kitchen. He said it looked serious."

[ANIMATION: Close-up of Aijan's face in the cold air, listening intently. Her eyes searching.]

Another pause.

"He wants to bring you a sign he had painted. He found someone who paints in the old style — the way market signs used to look in Soviet times. It says Аижандын ашканасы. He wants to hang it on your wall."

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Aijan stood in the cold for a long moment after the call ended.

Her father. Who had said nothing for eight months. Who had watched from a careful distance — the way Kyrgyz fathers of his generation watch the things they are not sure they approve of. Who had driven a marshrutka for twenty years and never started his own business.

He had gone to find someone who paints signs in the old Soviet market style. And he had ordered one painted with her name.

Аижандын ашканасы. Aijan's Kitchen.

[PAUSE]

She went back inside and sat down. The manty was almost gone. She made a note in her notebook, because she made notes in her notebook about everything now.

[ANIMATION: Notebook close-up. Handwriting appears: "Sign coming. Hang it in the kitchen. Show Gulnara."]

"Sign coming. Hang it in the kitchen. Show Gulnara."

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

That sign, painted in the old style by someone her father found, is not a business asset. You will not find it on a balance sheet or a business model canvas. But it represents something no framework can measure — the moment when the people who love you most see what you have built and quietly say: this is real.

That is what twelve weeks of discipline, testing, learning, and persistence produces. Not just a business. A new story about who you are and what you are capable of.
