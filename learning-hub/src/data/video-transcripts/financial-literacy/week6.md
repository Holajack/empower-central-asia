## Day 1: The Multiplication Effect

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Week 6 -- the final week. And I want to start by saying something directly to you. If you've made it this far, you are not the same person who started this course six weeks ago. You know things now that most people never learn. You've done things most people never do. And this week, we're going to talk about what you do with all of it.

Because here's the thing. Everything you've learned -- the budgeting, the debt snowball, the emergency fund, compound interest, generosity -- none of it was meant to stay locked up in one life. This week is about multiplication. Taking what you've received and passing it on.

[PAUSE]

There's a principle woven into the fabric of human progress: what you've received freely, give freely. It shows up in teaching, in parenting, in every form of mentorship. You learn something valuable, and then you turn around and pass it on. But most people stop there. They see teaching as a one-to-one transaction. That's addition. What we're talking about here is multiplication.

[GRAPHIC: "The Multiplication Math:
You teach 2 people
→ They each teach 2 more = 8 in Year 1
→ Year 2: 32
→ Year 3: 128
→ Year 4: 512
→ Year 5: 4,000+
If each impacts a household of 4: 16,000+ lives"]

[ON CAMERA]

Let me show you the math, because it's staggering. You complete this course. You commit to teaching just two people what you've learned. Those two people each teach two more. In one year, that's eight people. In two years, thirty-two. In three years, 128. In four years, 512. In five years, over 4,000 people. If each of those people impacts a household of four, you've indirectly affected over 16,000 lives. From one person taking one course.

[PAUSE]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Now scale it up further. If every person who completes this curriculum leads a small group of eight people, and every one of those eight goes on to lead their own group of eight, the numbers become almost unbelievable. One facilitator leads eight. Those eight each lead eight -- that's 64 in year two. Those 64 each lead groups -- 512 in year three. By year five, over 32,000 people have gone through the material.

Not through a bestselling book. Not through a massive marketing campaign. Through ordinary people willing to share what they've learned with the people right in front of them.

[B-ROLL: Ripples spreading outward in water, transitioning to a network visualization showing dots connecting to more dots]

[ON CAMERA]

This isn't theory. This is how movements actually spread. Alcoholics Anonymous started with one recovered alcoholic telling his story in a church basement. Someone heard it, got sober, and started another meeting in another town. That person influenced ten more, who influenced a hundred more. Today, AA operates in over 180 countries with two million members. It didn't start with funding or celebrities. It started with one person telling another: "I've been where you are, and there's a way out."

Financial literacy works the same way. You're not trying to reach 32,000 people. You're trying to reach two. Maybe it's your spouse and a friend. Maybe it's your kids and a coworker. Maybe it's a neighbor who mentioned they're drowning in debt and a cousin who just got their first credit card. You don't need a stage or a platform. You just need a kitchen table and the willingness to say, "Let me show you what I learned."

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

And here's what makes multiplication so powerful: it doesn't rely on experts. In fact, it works better without them. The person who just learned to budget last month is often a better teacher than the financial advisor who's been doing it for twenty years. Why? Because they remember what it felt like to not know. They remember the shame, the confusion, the fear. They speak the language of someone still figuring it out. That relatability is what creates trust. And trust is what creates change.

The movement doesn't need you to be perfect. It needs you to be honest. It needs you to say, "I paid off $3,000 in debt in six months, and here's exactly how I did it." Or, "I used to overdraft my account every month, and now I have a $1,000 emergency fund -- let me show you the steps." Your story -- with all its messy, imperfect, still-in-progress reality -- is more powerful than any polished presentation.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Think about it. Who in your life has been watching your transformation? Maybe they haven't said anything. But they've noticed. They've noticed that you seem less stressed. That you stopped complaining about money. That you're making different decisions. Someone is watching you right now, wondering if change is really possible. And they're waiting for someone to show them the way.

You are that someone. Not someday. Today.

## Day 2: Teaching What You've Learned

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Yesterday we talked about the power of multiplication. Today, I want to tackle the thing that stops most people from actually doing it. It's a lie. A really convincing lie that sounds like wisdom. And it goes like this: "I'm not qualified to teach anyone else."

[PAUSE]

Have you said that to yourself yet? "I don't know enough." "I'm still figuring this out." "Who am I to teach someone about money when I still have debt?" "I need to get my own house in order first."

I get it. Those feelings are real. But they're based on a false premise -- the idea that you have to be an expert before you're allowed to help anyone.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Think about the last time you learned something difficult. A new skill, a new piece of software, a new recipe. Who was more helpful to you: the person who mastered it twenty years ago and forgot what it's like to be a beginner, or the person who learned it six months ago and still remembers exactly where they got stuck? The recent learner knows which step is confusing. They know which explanation actually makes sense and which one sounds good but doesn't help. They know where the shortcuts are and where the pitfalls hide.

Expertise creates distance. Recent experience creates connection.

[B-ROLL: Two people at a table, one showing the other how to fill out a budget worksheet, pointing at specific lines, both leaning in]

[ON CAMERA]

When it comes to financial discipleship, the person who just finished paying off their first credit card is uniquely qualified to help someone drowning in debt. Not because they have all the answers, but because they remember the feeling. They remember the shame of opening the mail and seeing another past-due notice. They remember the arguments about money. They remember the desperation and the small, fragile hope that maybe things could be different. That memory is what makes them credible.

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

You don't need to be a certified financial planner to teach someone how to build a budget. You don't need an MBA to explain the debt snowball. You don't need a psychology degree to help someone identify their money scripts. You just need to be one step ahead and willing to turn around and extend a hand to the person behind you.

One step ahead. That's the qualification.

[PAUSE]

So how do you actually do it? Let me walk you through it.

[GRAPHIC: "How to Teach What You've Learned:
1. Start with YOUR story
2. Give them a framework (not a lecture)
3. One action step per week
4. Check in regularly
5. Show up -- presence matters most"]

[ON CAMERA]

Step one: you start with your story. Not a polished, scripted testimony. The real one. The one where you talk about the pile of bills on the kitchen table. The sick feeling in your stomach when you checked your bank balance. The moment you realized something had to change. You tell them where you started, what you did, and where you are now. You show them your actual budget. You walk them through your debt snowball. You let them see the process, not just the results.

Step two: you give them a framework. Not a lecture. A framework. You teach them the four walls -- food, shelter, transportation, basic clothing. You show them how to build a zero-based budget. You explain the debt snowball method. You help them calculate their emergency fund target. Don't overwhelm them with everything at once. Give them one thing to do this week. Next week, check in. Did they do it? If not, why? What got in the way? How can they adjust?

Step three: one action step at a time. Don't dump six weeks of material on someone in one conversation. Give them one thing. "This week, track every dollar you spend." That's it. Next week: "Now let's put those numbers into a budget." Small steps. Steady progress.

Step four: check in regularly. Send a text. Make a call. Meet for coffee. This isn't a one-time conversation. It's a relationship. The most effective mentors aren't the ones with the best material. They're the ones who keep showing up.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

And step five -- the most important one: show up. Be present. Answer the panicked text at 9 PM when they're about to make an impulse purchase. Celebrate when they pay off their first $500. Listen when they admit they've been lying to their spouse about spending. Don't judge. Don't lecture. Remember that six months ago, or six weeks ago, that was you. And someone showed up for you.

There's a role many people play without realizing it. The pioneer. The person who goes first. Who tests the path. Who comes back and says, "I made it through, and you can too." Every person who walks out of debt, who builds an emergency fund, who breaks a generational cycle of financial chaos -- that person is a pioneer. Not because they did something extraordinary, but because they did something first. And now someone else is watching.

You don't have to be perfect to be a pioneer. You just have to keep moving forward and be willing to tell the truth about how hard it was and how worth it it is.

[PAUSE]

If you taught just one person this year, who would it be? Name them. Right now, in your head. Because that person -- they need what you have. And you're ready.

## Day 3: Your 90-Day Plan & Financial Integrity

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

All right. This is the final lesson of the entire course. Everything has been building to this. And I'm going to cover two things today that might seem different but are deeply connected. First, your 90-day plan -- the specific, measurable road map for what happens after this course ends. And second, financial integrity -- the foundation that everything else has to stand on.

Let's start with the plan.

[PAUSE]

You've spent six weeks building new habits, shifting your mindset, and creating a financial strategy. Now comes the critical question: what happens when this course is over?

Here's the honest truth. Most people finish a program like this feeling energized and hopeful. And then life happens. The momentum fades. The budget gets abandoned. The debt snowball stalls. Within three months, they're back where they started. Not because the principles didn't work, but because they didn't have a plan for what comes next.

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

That's why you need a 90-day plan. Not a vague hope that you'll "keep doing better." A specific, measurable, time-bound plan with clear milestones.

[GRAPHIC: "Your 90-Day Plan:
- Specific: 'Pay off $1,800 on Visa' not 'pay off some debt'
- Measurable: exact dollar amounts and dates
- Time-bound: milestones at 30, 60, and 90 days
- Written down and posted somewhere visible
- Reviewed weekly"]

[ON CAMERA]

In the next 90 days, you will pay off X dollars of debt. You will save Y dollars for your emergency fund. You will give Z dollars. You will have a budget meeting with your spouse or accountability partner every Sunday at 7 PM. You will track every dollar. You will say no to three specific spending temptations. You write it down. You put it somewhere visible. And every week, you check your progress.

Here's what makes 90 days powerful. It's short enough to stay motivated and long enough to see real results. Ninety days is roughly three months -- enough time to pay off a small debt, build the beginning of an emergency fund, establish a new habit. It's long enough that you can't fake it. You actually have to do the work. And it's short enough that the finish line feels reachable. You're not committing to a lifetime of austerity. You're committing to three focused months. And then, at the end of those 90 days, you build another 90-day plan. And another. And another. That's how sustainable change happens.

[PAUSE]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Now. Before you build that plan, there's something even more foundational we need to address. And this is the part where I'm going to be direct with you, because I care more about your long-term success than your short-term comfort.

Integrity.

All the budgeting, all the debt payoff, all the savings and giving in the world won't matter if your financial foundation is built on dishonesty. Financial integrity is the bedrock everything else stands on. Without it, the whole structure eventually collapses. So before you multiply, before you teach, before you move forward, you need to take an honest inventory of five critical areas.

[GRAPHIC: "5 Areas of Financial Integrity:
1. Taxes
2. Work
3. Business Dealings
4. Debt
5. Relationships"]

[ON CAMERA]

Area one: taxes. Are you completely honest on your tax returns? Do you report all your income? Do you claim deductions you're not entitled to? Do you pay what you owe? Tax evasion feels victimless -- like you're just keeping more of your own money. But it's dishonesty. And dishonesty in one area always bleeds into others.

Area two: work. Do you give a full day's work for a full day's pay? Do you waste hours on personal tasks during work time? Do you pad your hours? Do you take supplies that aren't yours? Work integrity isn't just about not stealing. It's about honoring the trust placed in you.

Area three: business dealings. Are you truthful in all your transactions? Do you keep your promises? Do you deliver what you said you would? Do you manipulate contracts or exploit loopholes? This is where your reputation is built or destroyed. A life built on dishonest dealings might succeed for a season. But it always gets found out.

[PAUSE]

Area four: debt. Are you honoring your financial commitments? If you can't pay, are you communicating honestly with your creditors, or are you avoiding calls and letting accounts go to collections? Debt integrity means taking responsibility for what you owe and doing everything in your power to repay it. You made a promise. You keep it.

Area five: relationships. Are you financially transparent with your spouse and family? Do you hide purchases? Do you keep secret accounts? Do you lie about how much something cost? Financial dishonesty in a marriage is one of the leading causes of divorce. Not because of the money itself, but because of the broken trust.

[SERIOUS TONE]

[PAUSE]

Integrity costs something in the short term. Honesty might mean paying more in taxes. It might mean admitting to your spouse that you've been hiding spending. It might mean calling a creditor to set up a payment plan instead of ignoring the debt. It might mean losing a sale because you refused to lie.

But dishonesty costs everything in the long term. It costs your reputation, your relationships, your peace of mind, and ultimately your freedom. Your financial witness is established one honest decision at a time.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

So here's what I want you to do. Look at those five areas. Which one is your weakest? Be honest with yourself. And this week, take one step to strengthen it. Just one. Maybe it's a conversation with your spouse. Maybe it's an honest look at your tax situation. Maybe it's a phone call to a creditor you've been avoiding.

Then build your 90-day plan. Write three specific goals. Write the action steps. Write the dates. Put it somewhere you'll see it every day. And find someone who will hold you accountable.

Because here's what I believe: finishing well matters more than starting well. A lot of people start strong. But the test of character isn't how you begin. It's how you keep going when it's hard, when nobody's watching, when the results are slow. Legacy isn't just what you leave behind when you die. It's what you build into others while you live. And the most powerful legacy you can leave isn't money. It's character.

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]
