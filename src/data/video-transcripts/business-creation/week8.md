## Day 1: The 60-Second Pitch

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Week 8. This is where you learn to open your mouth and make people care.

[PAUSE]

Somewhere between your idea and your first customer -- or your first investor, or your first loan -- is a moment of communication. A moment when another person decides whether your business is worth their money, their time, or their trust. That moment is the pitch.

And most entrepreneurs are deeply unprepared for it. Not because they do not know their business. But because they confuse knowing something with being able to explain it clearly.

[GRAPHIC: Quote card -- "The ability to simplify means to eliminate the unnecessary so that the necessary may speak." -- Hans Hofmann]

This week, you will build three versions of your pitch -- sixty seconds, five minutes, and a structured narrative for handling objections. And you will test them against real human reactions.

We start today with the most distilled, most powerful version: the 60-second pitch.

[ON CAMERA]

Most entrepreneurs make the same fundamental mistake when they first try to pitch: they start with what they built.

[SERIOUS TONE]

They say, "I have a service that does X" or "I make a product that does Y," and then they wait for the listener to feel excited. But human beings do not get excited about products. They get excited about solutions to problems they recognize as real.

The structure of a compelling 60-second pitch is not about your solution at all. It starts with your listener's world, not yours.

[ON CAMERA]

There are four elements. Let me walk you through each one.

[GRAPHIC: "Element 1: THE HOOK" -- one sentence]

The first element is the hook -- a single sentence that grabs attention and earns the right to keep talking. A great hook is specific, surprising, or emotionally resonant.

[GRAPHIC: Good vs. Bad -- "Did you know that 70% of small business owners in Bishkek have never filed a proper tax return -- not because they are dishonest, but because no one has explained the system in their language?" vs. "I have a financial services business."]

"Did you know that seventy percent of small business owners in Bishkek have never filed a proper tax return -- not because they are dishonest, but because no one has ever explained the system in their language?" That is a hook. "I have a financial services business" is not.

The hook's only job is to make the listener lean forward.

[ON CAMERA]

[GRAPHIC: "Element 2: THE PROBLEM" -- two to three sentences]

The second element is the problem statement. This is where you earn credibility by demonstrating that you understand the pain your customer experiences better than they do themselves.

The problem statement should be specific enough to exclude people the problem does not affect. That specificity actually builds trust rather than shrinking your audience.

[GRAPHIC: Example -- "Families celebrating milestones in our neighborhood cannot find a reliable, custom cake service that is both beautiful and affordable. Either they settle for dry factory cakes from the supermarket or they pay two to three times what is reasonable at a formal bakery."]

That is a real problem statement. It names who is affected. It names the current alternatives. It names why those alternatives fail.

[ON CAMERA]

[GRAPHIC: "Element 3: THE SOLUTION" -- two to three sentences]

The third element is the solution. Not your product -- your solution. There is an important difference. Your product is what you built. Your solution is the mechanism by which a specific problem gets resolved.

The solution statement should feel almost inevitable given the problem you just described -- as if the listener could have arrived at it themselves.

[GRAPHIC: Example -- "I offer custom celebration cakes for 500 som, with two-day lead time and neighborhood delivery, using only real cream and fresh fruit."]

That solves the problem above in a way that feels logical, not salesy.

[ON CAMERA]

[GRAPHIC: "Element 4: THE ASK" -- one sentence, specific]

The fourth element is the ask. And this is where most pitches fall apart.

[SERIOUS TONE]

"I would love to connect sometime" is not an ask. It is a wish. "I am looking for three early customers who will give me honest feedback in exchange for a thirty percent discount on their first order" -- that is an ask. "I am raising two hundred thousand som to purchase a commercial oven and double my monthly capacity" -- that is an ask.

The ask must be specific. It must be easy for the right person to act on. And it must give the listener a clear next step.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, stringing these four elements together takes more practice than you expect.

[GRAPHIC: Framework summary -- "Hook (1 sentence) --> Problem (2-3 sentences) --> Solution (2-3 sentences) --> Ask (1 sentence). Total: under 60 seconds."]

Your first version will probably take three minutes. It will meander. It will include things that feel important to you but mean nothing to your listener. That is normal.

The process of editing a pitch down to sixty seconds is not just a communication exercise. It is a thinking exercise. Every sentence you cut forces you to clarify which parts of your business actually matter most. The compression reveals the core.

[ON CAMERA]

Pacing and delivery matter as much as content. A pitch delivered too fast sounds nervous. Too slow sounds unsure. The ideal pace leaves room for the listener to process what they just heard before the next idea arrives.

And here is the most important thing about practice.

[PAUSE]

Reading your pitch is not practicing your pitch. Recording yourself and playing it back is not practicing your pitch.

Delivering it to another human being and watching their face -- that is practicing your pitch.

The feedback you need is almost entirely nonverbal. Did their eyes light up or glaze over? Did they interrupt with a question -- that is good -- or wait politely to escape?

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

There is a final principle. The goal of the 60-second pitch is not to convince anyone of anything. It is to earn a second conversation. If someone listens and says, "Tell me more about that," you have succeeded. If they walk away with a follow-up plan, you have succeeded.

[ON CAMERA]

Practice your pitch until it feels like a story you are telling, not a script you are reciting. The moment it stops sounding rehearsed and starts sounding natural is the moment it starts working.

That transition usually requires somewhere between twenty and fifty repetitions. Most founders stop at five. Do not be most founders.

[GRAPHIC: Day 1 Action -- "Write your 60-second pitch using the hook-problem-solution-ask framework. Time yourself. If it runs over 75 seconds, cut from the solution section. Then deliver it out loud to one person."]

Write it. Time it. Cut it. Deliver it. See you tomorrow.

## Day 2: The 5-Minute Story

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 2. Yesterday you built a sixty-second pitch. Today we expand it.

[PAUSE]

When someone gives you five minutes, the rules change. In sixty seconds, you are earning attention. In five minutes, you are earning trust.

The listener has chosen to invest real time in your idea. Which means they want more than a hook and a problem. They want evidence. They want to know this is a real opportunity. That you understand the landscape. That you have some proof the idea works. And that you are the right person to execute it.

[GRAPHIC: "The 5-Minute Pitch" title card -- expanded elements listed]

[ON CAMERA]

The 5-minute pitch opens with the same hook-problem-solution foundation as the sixty-second version, but expanded. You take ninety seconds where before you took thirty. You use that additional time to deepen the problem framing with specific data or vivid customer examples.

[GRAPHIC: Comparison -- 60-second: "Families cannot find affordable custom cakes." vs. 5-minute: "Last month I spoke with eleven families planning celebrations. Nine had stopped ordering custom cakes entirely because the last one cost 2,000 som and arrived late. They are not price insensitive -- they are burned."]

See the difference? The specificity transforms the problem from abstract to real.

[ON CAMERA]

The next element to add is market context. How large is the opportunity?

[SERIOUS TONE]

Market sizing is one of the most commonly fumbled parts of any pitch. Founders either pick an enormous global number that has no relationship to their actual reach, or they get so granular that the market sounds too small to matter.

The most credible approach is what is called the "bottoms-up" method.

[GRAPHIC: Bottoms-up market sizing -- "3,000 households in 2km radius x 5% ordering rate x 2 cakes per year x 600 som each = 180,000 som annually"]

Start with your actual potential customer count in your actual geography. Multiply by realistic purchase frequency and transaction size. Present that as your realistic near-term opportunity.

"There are approximately three thousand households within a two-kilometer radius of my kitchen. If five percent of them order two celebration cakes per year at six hundred som each, my addressable market is one hundred and eighty thousand som annually." That is credible. "The global cake market is forty-five billion dollars" is not.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, the most powerful element of any 5-minute pitch -- and the most underused by early founders.

[GRAPHIC: "TRACTION" in large text]

Traction is evidence that the business is working. That real people are paying real money for something you built. It does not have to be large.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Five paying customers is traction. Twelve repeat orders is traction. A waiting list of thirty people is traction. An unsolicited testimonial from a customer who told three friends is traction.

The function of traction in a pitch is to shift your business from the theoretical to the proven -- even in a small way. Any evidence that real humans have encountered your business and chosen to exchange something valuable for it strengthens your pitch enormously.

[ON CAMERA]

The team section is where many founders undersell themselves. They say, "I am just starting out" or "it is just me right now," as if that is disqualifying.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Investors and lenders are not looking for a Fortune 500 org chart. They are looking for evidence that you have the relevant knowledge, commitment, and character to execute. If you have worked in the industry, say so. If you have successfully sold something before, say so. If you have a relevant skill -- accounting, cooking, construction, design -- connect it to why this business is within your capability.

The question the listener is asking is "why this person?" Make sure your pitch answers it.

[ON CAMERA]

Guy Kawasaki, a venture capitalist who reviewed thousands of pitches, proposed the 10/20/30 rule for presentation pitches: ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point minimum font.

[GRAPHIC: "10/20/30 Rule -- The underlying principle: complexity in presentation is almost always a sign of unclear thinking, not depth of knowledge."]

The founder who needs sixty slides to explain their business usually understands it less clearly than the one who can do it in ten. For a verbal 5-minute pitch, the equivalent principle is this: if you cannot explain your business model in two sentences, you do not yet understand your business model well enough.

[ON CAMERA]

The business model section answers the question every listener is silently asking: how does money flow through this thing?

This does not require a financial model. It requires clarity.

[GRAPHIC: Example -- "I sell custom cakes for 500-800 som each. My variable cost is 280 som. I need to sell 90 cakes per month to cover fixed costs. I am currently at 40, with three months of runway."]

"I sell custom cakes for five hundred to eight hundred som each. My variable cost per cake is two hundred and eighty som. I need to sell ninety cakes per month to cover my fixed costs, and I am currently at forty per month with three months of runway."

[PAUSE]

That level of financial clarity in a pitch signals something important: this founder knows their numbers. And after last week, you do too.

[ON CAMERA]

The 5-minute pitch ends with an ask that is larger and more specific than the sixty-second version allows.

[GRAPHIC: Specific ask example -- "I am looking for a 150,000 som loan to purchase a professional oven and extend my kitchen rental to six days. At that capacity I can produce 120 cakes per month, above my break-even of 90. I am projecting repayment within 18 months based on my current growth rate."]

"I am looking for a one hundred and fifty thousand som loan to purchase a professional oven and extend my kitchen rental to six days per week. At that capacity I can produce one hundred and twenty cakes per month, which is above my break-even of ninety. I am projecting repayment within eighteen months."

That tells the listener exactly what you need, exactly what it buys, and exactly how you intend to pay it back.

[ON CAMERA]

Practice this version with a timer running. Your first attempt will take nine minutes. Cutting it to five will be painful -- you will feel like you are losing important information. But the discipline of cutting almost always produces a stronger, clearer narrative.

[GRAPHIC: Day 2 Action -- "Build your 5-minute pitch adding market size, traction, team, business model, and specific ask. Time yourself. Cut to 5 minutes."]

What you cut was probably not as essential as it felt. See you tomorrow.

## Day 3: Handling Questions and Objections

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 3. You have built two versions of your pitch -- sixty seconds and five minutes. Today we prepare you for what happens after the pitch ends.

Because the most revealing part of any pitch is not the presentation. It is the Q&A.

[GRAPHIC: "Handling Questions & Objections" title card]

[ON CAMERA]

The most important thing to understand about objections is this: they are not attacks. They are questions wearing armor.

[PAUSE]

When an investor says "the market seems too small," they are not dismissing your business. They are asking you to help them understand why the opportunity is larger or more durable than it first appears.

When a family member says "you love baking, but can you actually make money?" they are not doubting you. They are expressing a legitimate concern about financial risk -- one they are carrying emotionally because they care about you.

Recognizing the real question beneath the objection is the first step to answering it well.

[ON CAMERA]

The most common objections fall into six categories, and I want you to be prepared for all of them.

[GRAPHIC: Six objection types listed vertically]

Market objections: "There is not enough demand." "The market is saturated." These are best answered with specific customer evidence and bottoms-up market data. Not by arguing about global industry size.

Competitive objections: "There are already established players doing this." Answer by articulating your specific advantage and naming the exact customer segment that existing players are failing to serve.

Credibility objections: "Why you specifically?" Connect your personal background, industry experience, or relevant skills directly to the execution requirements of this business.

[ON CAMERA]

Timing objections: "This market is not ready yet" or "someone bigger will come in and crush you." These require honest engagement with the timing argument. Sometimes the objector has identified a real risk.

Financial objections: "Your margins seem too thin" or "that growth projection seems unrealistic." These require transparent access to your actual data. Open your Financial Dashboard. Show them.

And relationship objections -- especially common in Central Asian contexts: "What happens if this does not work and you cannot repay me?"

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Now, here is the single most important principle of handling objections.

[GRAPHIC: Large text -- "'I don't know, but I will find out and follow up by Thursday' builds more trust than a confident-sounding answer you made up."]

The most damaging response to any objection is to pretend you have an answer when you do not. "I do not know, but I will find out and follow up by Thursday" is not a weakness. It is a demonstration of intellectual honesty and follow-through commitment.

Listeners who have evaluated many pitches have reliable instincts for when founders are making things up. The response that sounds uncertain but honest almost always lands better than the response that sounds certain but hollow.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Now, I want to talk about something specific to your context. Pitching to family members.

In many Kyrgyz, Kazakh, and Uzbek families, asking for a loan -- even for a well-reasoned business purpose -- activates a complex set of relational dynamics. The family member may fear saying no and damaging the relationship. They may worry about the social implications of lending money that is not repaid. They may feel exposed to criticism from other family members if the venture fails.

Understanding these dynamics does not mean manipulating them. It means acknowledging them directly.

[ON CAMERA]

When pitching to a family lender, the most effective approach is to separate the relationship conversation from the business conversation explicitly.

[GRAPHIC: Key phrase -- "I want to show you the business case. And I also want to say upfront: if it does not make sense financially for you, I will completely understand and it will not affect our relationship."]

Say it. Mean it. That one sentence removes the relational pressure and allows the family member to evaluate the opportunity on its merits.

And presenting a simple one-page written summary -- even handwritten -- signals seriousness. It creates a record that honors the formality of a financial request, regardless of the informal setting.

[ON CAMERA]

The practice of handling objections is genuinely a physical skill, not just an intellectual one.

You must practice responding out loud, in real time, under mild social pressure, to develop the composure that makes objection-handling appear natural. The founder who has mentally prepared three answers but has never spoken them aloud will still fumble when a real human fires the objection unexpectedly.

Practice means doing it out loud. With another person present. Someone who will ask follow-up questions and not let you off the hook easily.

[PAUSE]

And keep track of every objection you receive. Build a simple log: what was the objection, how did you respond, and did the response satisfy the listener? Over time this log reveals patterns -- certain objections appear repeatedly, which means your pitch is failing to pre-answer a common concern. The best pitches incorporate the most important objections into the pitch itself before they can be raised.

[ON CAMERA]

Before we close, a deeper reflection.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

There is a version of pitching that is fundamentally about performance -- managing impressions, controlling information, projecting confidence even when it is not warranted. That version tends to produce short-term results and long-term damage to trust.

There is another version that is fundamentally about honest representation -- making the clearest possible case for something you genuinely believe in, while being truthful about what you do not know.

The founders who build businesses that last in community-based economies -- where reputation travels faster than any marketing campaign -- almost universally land in the second category. Their pitches succeed not because they are more polished, but because they are more honest. When they say "I do not know," people believe them when they say "I am confident." When they acknowledge a risk, people trust their assessment of opportunities.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Your pitch is not just a business communication. It is a statement about the kind of person you are and the kind of business you intend to run.

[GRAPHIC: Reflection Questions -- "Is there anything in your pitch that you say with more confidence than you actually feel?" and "How do you want to be remembered by the first people who invested in you -- as a good pitch-maker, or as a person who delivered on what they promised?"]

[PAUSE]

Sit with those questions.

[GRAPHIC: Day 3 Action -- "Practice handling at least 3 common objections OUT LOUD. Not in writing. Out loud. With another person present. Until the responses feel natural."]

Practice out loud. We need the words in your mouth, not just in your head. See you tomorrow.

## Day 4: Story -- Aijan Pitches to Her Uncle

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Story

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Day 4. Today is story day. And this one is about the conversation Aijan had been building toward for eight weeks.

[PAUSE]

Aijan had spent three days rewriting her pitch. She had gone through four different versions.

[ANIMATION: Four crumpled pieces of paper on a desk, with a fifth, clean sheet being written.]

The first was basically a cake recipe. The second was a financial spreadsheet that made her eyes cross when she read it back. The third was a heartfelt story about how much she loved baking -- her mentor told her it was "lovely but not investable."

The fourth version was tighter.

[ON CAMERA]

Her uncle Bakyt was a careful man.

[B-ROLL: Older man in a modest shop, reviewing paperwork]

He had run a small hardware import business for fourteen years. He had seen enough optimistic relatives with confident plans to develop a healthy skepticism about anything that sounded too good. He agreed to hear her out over tea. Which felt both better and worse than a formal meeting.

[ANIMATION: Aijan and Bakyt at a small table. Two cups of tea. Bakyt wraps both hands around his cup.]

"Go ahead," he said.

[ON CAMERA]

Aijan took a breath.

[ANIMATION: Aijan speaking. Speech bubble appearing word by word: "In our neighborhood, there is no reliable custom cake service for small celebrations -- birthdays, engagements, graduations. People either buy a factory cake from the supermarket that tastes like cardboard, or they pay 2,000 som at a formal bakery for something they could have gotten for 500 if there were another option."]

She paused.

[ANIMATION: Aijan, steady. Speech bubble: "I am that other option."]

[ON CAMERA]

Bakyt looked at her over his tea.

[ANIMATION: Bakyt. Speech bubble: "You love baking. But can you actually make money?"]

She had expected this. She had practiced for this.

[ANIMATION: Aijan sliding a single handwritten sheet across the table. Speech bubble: "I ran the numbers."]

"At six hundred som per cake with my current costs, I break even at fifty cakes a month. I am currently at fifteen, with no marketing at all. I am asking for eighty thousand som to buy a second-hand stand mixer and extend my kitchen rental to five days a week. That lets me handle sixty cakes a month, which puts me above break-even."

[ANIMATION: Bakyt studying the sheet. Turning it over. Looking back at Aijan.]

Bakyt studied the sheet for a long time.

"What if it does not work?"

[ANIMATION: Aijan, calm. Speech bubble: "Then I return what I have left and we have an honest conversation. I am not asking you to believe it will definitely work. I am asking you to look at the math and tell me if the plan is reasonable."]

[ON CAMERA]

[PAUSE]

He was quiet for a long moment. He turned the paper over, as if checking for fine print on the back.

[ANIMATION: Bakyt setting down his cup. Speech bubble: "You did not say what I expected you to say."]

"What did you expect?" Aijan asked.

[ANIMATION: Bakyt. Speech bubble: "That your cakes are the best, that everyone loves them, that this is your passion."]

He looked up.

[ANIMATION: Bakyt. Speech bubble: "You said the problem first. That was different."]

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Aijan nodded. "My mentor told me investors do not care about passion. They care about problems. The passion is what makes me get up at five in the morning to solve the problem. But that is my business, not theirs."

[ANIMATION: Bakyt picking up his cup again. A small nod. Speech bubble: "Come back Saturday with a more detailed repayment plan. And bring a cake. I will have my wife tell me what she thinks of it."]

[PAUSE]

It was not a yes. But it was a Saturday.

And Saturday felt very much like a beginning.

[GRAPHIC: Key Takeaway -- "Lead with the problem, not the passion. Show the math, not just the dream. Acknowledge risk honestly. Earn the next conversation -- that is all a pitch needs to do."]

That is Aijan's pitch story. Tomorrow we put yours together. See you then.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Day 4. Today's story is about a conversation at a hardware shop, a container of manty, and a handwritten sheet of numbers.

[PAUSE]

Aijan had spent three evenings rewriting her pitch. The first version was a story about her family -- her father's back, her brother's university fund, her mother's recipes from Tokmok. Every word was true. But her facilitator told her it would move a relative, not a lender.

[ANIMATION: Facilitator speaking to Aijan. Speech bubble: "Tell them the problem first. Your story is your credibility, not your opening."]

"Tell them the problem first," he said. "Your story is your credibility, not your opening."

[ON CAMERA]

Her father's older brother, Kanybek, was the only person in the family with money to lend.

[B-ROLL: Small hardware shop with imported goods on shelves]

He had run a small hardware import business for twelve years, buying from suppliers in China and selling to construction shops in Bishkek. He was a careful man who had seen three relatives ask him for loans in the past five years and repaid him a total of zero.

[ANIMATION: Kanybek's face -- weathered, patient, skeptical. The face of a man who has learned to say no.]

He agreed to hear her out over tea at his shop. Which felt both more and less intimidating than a formal meeting.

[ON CAMERA]

Aijan arrived with two things: a single handwritten sheet with her numbers, and a container of manty she had made that morning. She set the manty on the table without mentioning it and let Kanybek pour the tea.

[ANIMATION: Aijan and Kanybek at a small table in the back of the hardware shop. Tools on shelves behind them. Tea being poured.]

"Go ahead," he said.

[ANIMATION: Aijan taking a breath. Speech bubble: "The offices around Manas Avenue and Chui have thousands of workers who eat lunch every day. The ashkana in those buildings reheats yesterday's food. There is no reliable, fresh home-cooked option delivered to desks by 12:30. I am building that option."]

She paused.

[ANIMATION: Aijan. Speech bubble: "I have been running it for six weeks. I have twenty-two regular customers and a Telegram channel that adds subscribers every week."]

[ON CAMERA]

Kanybek looked at her over his cup.

[ANIMATION: Kanybek. Speech bubble: "Women in your family cook well. Can you actually make money?"]

She had expected this.

[ANIMATION: Aijan sliding the handwritten sheet across the table. Speech bubble: "I ran the numbers."]

"My variable cost per portion is eighty-five som. I charge one hundred and seventy. My fixed costs are six thousand som a month. I break even at seventy-one portions per month. I am currently at fifty-five."

[ANIMATION: Close-up on the handwritten sheet -- neat numbers, clean layout, simple math.]

"I am asking for forty thousand som to rent a small shared kitchen space for three months. That lets me increase production and take on the office building contracts I currently cannot fulfill because my apartment kitchen is too small."

[ON CAMERA]

Kanybek picked up the sheet. He studied it for a long time without speaking.

[ANIMATION: Kanybek examining the paper. Turning it over. Looking at the numbers again. This is how he studies things -- contracts, import documents, the faces of people asking him for something.]

"What if the offices do not keep ordering?" he asked.

[ANIMATION: Aijan, steady. Speech bubble: "Then I have three months of runway and I have not quit my accounting job yet. I am not asking you to believe it will definitely work. I am asking you to tell me whether the plan is reasonable enough to test with a small investment rather than a large one."]

[ON CAMERA]

[PAUSE]

He was quiet again. He opened the container of manty. Ate one. Nodded slightly -- not at her. At the manty.

[ANIMATION: Kanybek chewing, considering. Then speaking. Speech bubble: "Come back Thursday. Bring the contracts you are proposing to sign with those offices. And bring more manty. My wife needs to have an opinion too."]

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

It was not a yes. But Thursday felt like a door that had not been closed.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Aijan did. She led with the problem, not the passion. She showed real numbers -- handwritten, honest, traceable. She acknowledged risk before he could raise it. She did not ask him to believe in her dream. She asked him to evaluate whether the math made a small test worthwhile.

And she brought manty. Because in Kyrgyz culture, the food speaks before the founder does.

[GRAPHIC: Key Takeaway -- "Separate the relationship from the request. Show the math. Acknowledge risk. Keep the ask small enough to be reasonable. And let your product do some of the talking."]

## Day 5: Action -- Pitch Builder Workshop

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day 5. Today we build your pitch from the ground up. By the end of this session, you will have a written sixty-second pitch, the framework for a five-minute version, and a plan for testing both against real people.

Pull out your Pitch Builder worksheet. We are going section by section.

[GRAPHIC: "Pitch Builder" worksheet header]

[ON CAMERA]

Section one: Your 60-Second Pitch. Four fields, each building on the last.

Start with your hook. One sentence. Something specific or surprising that makes a listener lean forward. Remember: do not start with "I have a business that..." Start with their world, not yours.

Write your problem statement. Two to three sentences describing the pain your customer experiences. Be specific about who suffers this problem and why current solutions fail. The problem statement is more important than the solution. If the listener does not feel the problem, the solution will not matter.

Write your solution. Two to three sentences. Lead with the outcome your customer gets, not the features of your product.

Optionally, add a one-sentence market signal -- how big is the opportunity, using a specific local number. And a one-sentence business model signal -- who pays, how much, how often.

Then your ask. One sentence. Specific. Easy to act on. Not "I would love to connect." A number, a next step, a particular type of introduction.

[ON CAMERA]

Now time yourself reading the whole thing aloud. Aim for fifty-five to sixty-five seconds. If you are over seventy-five seconds, cut something -- probably from the solution.

[GRAPHIC: Timing target -- "55-65 seconds. Over 75? Cut from the solution."]

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Your 5-Minute Pitch expansion.

Add your market size using a bottoms-up calculation. Number of potential customers in your area, times purchase frequency, times transaction value. Use local numbers, not global statistics.

Add your traction -- every piece of real evidence that the business is working. Paying customers. Repeat orders. Testimonials. A waiting list. Name what you have.

Add your team and credibility. What makes you specifically suited to execute this? Do not undersell yourself.

Add your business model explanation. Who pays, how much, how often, what is your margin, what is your break-even -- in three to four sentences.

And add your specific ask with use of funds. Amount requested, exactly what it enables, projected outcome, and repayment plan.

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Pitch checklist. Before any real pitch conversation, confirm these seven items.

[GRAPHIC: Pitch Checklist -- seven items]

Does your pitch lead with the problem, not the product? Does it contain at least three specific numbers? Does it include at least one piece of real traction? Is your ask specific -- a number and a next step? Have you rehearsed it out loud at least ten times? Have you prepared honest responses to at least three common objections? And can you state your CAC, LTV, break-even, and runway from memory?

If you cannot check all seven, you are not ready to pitch.

[ON CAMERA]

Section four: Objection Log. This is an ongoing document. Every time you pitch to someone and receive a question or objection, write it down. Record the exact words they used. Record how you responded. Record whether your response satisfied them. And record how you will incorporate this feedback into the next version of your pitch.

[GRAPHIC: Objection Log format -- "Objection received | My response | Was it satisfying? | How to incorporate into pitch"]

The objections that appear repeatedly are telling you something your pitch is not yet communicating. Listen to them.
