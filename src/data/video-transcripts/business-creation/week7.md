## Day 1: Unit Economics -- Does Your Business Math Work?

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Week 7. Welcome to Module 3: Validate Your Assumptions.

[PAUSE]

For the past two weeks, you have been shaping your business model and testing it against real customer feedback. You talked to people. You listened. You updated your canvas based on evidence. That work was essential.

But today we shift gears. Today we stop talking in feelings and start talking in numbers.

[GRAPHIC: Quote card -- "Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality." -- Unknown]

That quote says everything you need to know about this week. Many entrepreneurs know their product is good. Their customers say nice things. The revenue looks encouraging on a good month. But they have never done the math to find out if the business is actually financially viable.

This week, you will answer that question with certainty. By the end of the week, you will know whether your business math actually works -- or whether you need to change something fundamental before you can grow.

[ON CAMERA]

We start with the most powerful financial concept for early-stage businesses: unit economics.

[GRAPHIC: "Unit Economics" title card]

Unit economics strips away all the complexity and comes down to one question: on a single sale, do you make money or lose it?

Not after a great month. Not in some imagined future when you have scale. Right now. On the next transaction you complete. Does your business model generate value, or does it destroy it?

[SERIOUS TONE]

Most entrepreneurs are shocked by what they find when they actually calculate this.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me teach you the two numbers at the heart of unit economics.

[GRAPHIC: "CAC -- Customer Acquisition Cost"]

Customer Acquisition Cost -- CAC -- is the total amount you spend to bring in one new paying customer. This includes everything: marketing expenses, hours of sales time, social media ads, free samples you hand out at the bazaar -- all of it, divided by the number of new customers those efforts produced.

[GRAPHIC: Simple calculation -- "10,000 som spent on marketing last month / 20 new customers = 500 som CAC"]

If you spent ten thousand som last month on marketing and got twenty new customers, your CAC is five hundred som per customer. That is the price of growth. That number is not there to make you feel bad. It is there so you can compare it to the next number.

[ON CAMERA]

[GRAPHIC: "LTV -- Customer Lifetime Value"]

Customer Lifetime Value -- LTV -- is the total revenue you expect to earn from a single customer over the entire duration of your relationship with them.

For a one-time purchase business, LTV is simply the average sale amount minus the cost of goods sold.

But for a repeat-purchase business -- like a bakery, a cleaning service, a lunch delivery -- you multiply the average transaction value by the average number of purchases per year, then by the average number of years a customer stays with you.

[GRAPHIC: LTV formula -- "Average Transaction Value x Purchases Per Year x Customer Lifespan (years)"]

That calculation suddenly makes loyal customers look very different from random one-time buyers.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, the ratio between these two numbers is the single most important number in unit economics.

[GRAPHIC: "LTV : CAC Ratio" -- Large "3:1" with "Minimum healthy ratio" underneath]

As a general rule, your LTV should be at least three times your CAC. A three-to-one ratio.

If it is below three-to-one, you are spending more to acquire customers than those customers are worth to you.

[SERIOUS TONE]

And here is what many entrepreneurs miss: growing that business faster does not fix the problem. It accelerates it. A business losing money on every customer does not become profitable through volume. It becomes bankrupt faster.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me make this real with some Central Asian examples.

[B-ROLL: Market scene in Bishkek]

A seamstress in Bishkek charges fifteen hundred som for an alteration. She spends two hours posting on Instagram and visiting a cloth market to find one new customer. If we value her time honestly, her CAC includes the value of those hours.

But consider a tea vendor in Almaty who relies entirely on word-of-mouth. His CAC is nearly zero. And his LTV is enormous because his customers buy from him daily for years. These are radically different business models, and the unit economics of each tell completely different stories about sustainability.

[ON CAMERA]

Many entrepreneurs operating in som or tenge find these calculations feel abstract because they have been thinking in daily cash flow rather than per-customer profitability. The shift required is to start thinking about each customer as either making your business stronger or making it weaker.

[PAUSE]

The good news is you do not need complex software to calculate this. You need four things: your actual marketing spending for last month, the number of new customers it produced, your gross margin per sale, and an honest estimate of how often and how long your average customer stays with you.

Put those numbers in the worksheet and let them speak. Whatever they reveal is better information than the stories you have been telling yourself about how the business is doing.

[ON CAMERA]

If your LTV-to-CAC ratio is below three-to-one, you have three levers to pull.

[GRAPHIC: Three levers -- "1. Reduce CAC (find cheaper ways to reach customers)" "2. Increase transaction value (raise prices or add higher-margin options)" "3. Increase repeat purchases (loyalty programs, follow-up, retention)"]

You can reduce your acquisition costs -- find cheaper ways to reach customers. You can increase your average transaction value -- raise prices or introduce higher-margin add-ons. Or you can increase repeat purchase frequency -- create loyalty programs, follow up actively, give customers reasons to return.

The unit economics framework tells you which lever matters most based on your specific situation.

[GRAPHIC: Day 1 Action -- "Calculate your CAC and LTV using real numbers from the last 30 days. What is your ratio?"]

Do the math today. We are going to need these numbers tomorrow. See you then.

## Day 2: Break-Even Analysis -- When Will You Stop Losing Money?

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 2. Yesterday you calculated your unit economics -- the profit or loss on a single sale. Today we zoom out. We ask: when does the entire business stop losing money?

[GRAPHIC: "Break-Even Analysis" title card]

That moment -- the point where total revenue exactly equals total costs -- is called break-even. Below it, every transaction generates revenue but the business is still losing money in aggregate. Above it, every additional unit sold produces real profit.

Break-even is not a milestone you celebrate and forget. It is the threshold your business must cross every single month just to survive.

[ON CAMERA]

To calculate your break-even point, you first need to understand a fundamental distinction: fixed costs versus variable costs.

[GRAPHIC: Two columns -- "Fixed Costs" and "Variable Costs"]

Fixed costs are the expenses that do not change based on how much you sell. They exist whether you sell one unit or one thousand. Monthly rent. Phone plan. Website hosting. Loan repayments. The cost of keeping the lights on.

Variable costs are expenses that increase directly as you sell more. Raw materials. Packaging. Delivery fees. Commissions. If you sell twice as many cakes, you buy twice as much flour. That flour cost is variable.

[ON CAMERA]

The difference between your selling price and your variable cost per unit is called your contribution margin.

[GRAPHIC: "Selling Price - Variable Cost = Contribution Margin"]

This is the amount each sale contributes toward covering your fixed costs -- and eventually toward profit.

[GRAPHIC: Example -- "500 som (price) - 300 som (variable cost) = 200 som (contribution margin)"]

If you sell a product for five hundred som and your variable cost is three hundred som, your contribution margin is two hundred som. That two hundred does not go straight to profit. First, it must cover the fixed costs that exist regardless of how much you sell.

[ON CAMERA]

The break-even formula is straightforward.

[GRAPHIC: Large formula -- "Fixed Costs / Contribution Margin = Break-Even Units"]

Fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit equals break-even units.

[PAUSE]

Let me walk you through a real example.

[GRAPHIC: "Bakery Break-Even Example" with all numbers laid out]

A baker in Bishkek sells celebration cakes for five hundred som each. The ingredients and packaging -- her variable costs -- run three hundred som per cake. Her contribution margin is two hundred som.

Her fixed costs each month: a shared commercial kitchen at twelve thousand som, phone and internet at three thousand som, and marketing materials plus delivery costs at five thousand som. Total fixed costs: twenty thousand som per month.

[GRAPHIC: Calculation shown step by step -- "20,000 som / 200 som = 100 cakes per month"]

Twenty thousand divided by two hundred equals one hundred cakes per month. Until she sells the hundredth cake each month, she is operating at a loss. Cake one hundred and one is her first profitable sale.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, that number -- one hundred cakes -- is not discouraging. It is clarifying.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Without it, the baker might feel like any month with decent revenue is a good month. With it, she knows that eighty cakes feels busy but is still losing money. She knows that one hundred and twenty cakes feels about the same as one hundred but is actually profitable. She knows that every decision she makes -- about pricing, about costs, about how many orders she takes -- connects to that single threshold number.

[ON CAMERA]

Break-even analysis also reveals something powerful: the sensitivity of your business to cost changes.

[GRAPHIC: Scenario comparison -- "Kitchen rent goes from 12,000 to 16,000 som"]

If the commercial kitchen raises its rent from twelve thousand to sixteen thousand som, fixed costs jump to twenty-four thousand and the break-even rises to one hundred and twenty cakes. A thirty-three percent increase in one fixed cost requires a twenty percent increase in sales volume just to stay in the same position.

[GRAPHIC: Opposite scenario -- "Variable cost drops from 300 to 250 som"]

But if the baker negotiates better ingredient prices and drops her variable cost from three hundred to two hundred and fifty som per cake, her contribution margin rises to two hundred and fifty and her break-even drops to eighty cakes. Same volume of sales, but now she is profitable two months out of three instead of zero.

[ON CAMERA]

It is worth noting that break-even is dynamic, not static. As your business grows and takes on new fixed costs -- hiring an assistant, renting dedicated space, buying equipment -- your break-even point rises.

Growing a business without recalculating break-even is like adding weight to a plane without checking whether the engine can still take off. Many businesses that appear to be thriving are actually carrying more cost structure than their revenue can support. They discover this only when a slow month hits.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

The discipline of calculating break-even monthly creates a fundamentally different kind of business owner: one who knows at any point in the month whether they are above or below the threshold. One who understands which costs are worth cutting and which are worth bearing. One who makes pricing and volume decisions based on math rather than mood.

That discipline is what separates businesses that survive from businesses that limp along until they quietly die.

[GRAPHIC: Day 2 Action -- "Calculate YOUR break-even point. Selling price - variable cost = contribution margin. Fixed costs / contribution margin = break-even units. Are you above or below?"]

Do the calculation today. Bring your number to the group. See you tomorrow.

## Day 3: Burn Rate and Runway -- How Long Can You Survive?

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 3. Today we talk about the clock that is ticking on every early-stage business.

[PAUSE]

The question is not just whether your business model will eventually work. It is whether it will work before you run out of money.

[GRAPHIC: "Burn Rate & Runway" title card]

Burn rate and runway are the two numbers that answer that question with mathematical clarity. They are the most honest assessment of the urgency facing your business. And entrepreneurs who ignore them often discover the truth too late.

[ON CAMERA]

Burn rate is the net amount of money your business consumes each month.

[GRAPHIC: "Burn Rate = Monthly Spending - Monthly Revenue"]

If your business brings in eighty thousand som in revenue and spends ninety-five thousand som on all costs, your burn rate is fifteen thousand som per month. That is not an accounting technicality. That is the speed at which your financial reserves are shrinking.

[ON CAMERA]

Runway is calculated by dividing your current cash reserves by your monthly burn rate.

[GRAPHIC: "Runway = Cash on Hand / Monthly Burn Rate"]

If you have ninety thousand som saved and your burn rate is fifteen thousand som per month, your runway is six months.

[GRAPHIC: Visual timeline -- six months illustrated as a fuel gauge depleting]

That number represents your window of viability -- the amount of time you have to either fix the unit economics, reach break-even, find additional funding, or pivot to something that works better.

Six months can feel like a long time. Combined with the time it takes to iterate, test pricing changes, build customer relationships, and see results -- six months passes very quickly.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Here is the critical rule, and I need you to hear this clearly.

[GRAPHIC: Warning -- large red text -- "Under 3 months of runway: STOP EVERYTHING and fix this NOW."]

If you have fewer than three months of runway remaining, stop everything else and fix this immediately. Not eventually. Not after your next product launch. Not after your marketing push. Immediately.

A business with two months of runway is in crisis mode. The appropriate response to crisis mode is to cut every non-essential expense, pursue revenue with singular focus, and have honest conversations with anyone who might extend financial support.

[PAUSE]

The biggest mistake founders make with short runway is continuing normal operations while silently hoping the situation resolves itself. It will not resolve itself. The numbers do not care about hope.

[ON CAMERA]

For entrepreneurs in Central Asia who are often bootstrapping from personal savings or small family loans -- sometimes measured in thousands of som or tenge rather than tens of thousands -- these calculations are not abstract.

[B-ROLL: Young woman at a small desk, reviewing papers and counting cash]

A young woman starting a tutoring business with thirty thousand som of personal savings and a ten thousand som monthly burn rate has three months of runway. If she spends the first month building a perfect website rather than acquiring students, she has spent thirty-three percent of her runway on something that generates no immediate revenue.

The runway calculation makes that trade-off visible in a way that feelings never can.

[ON CAMERA]

Reducing burn rate means examining every cost with honest scrutiny. Some costs are genuinely necessary to operate: ingredients, basic communications, transportation. Others feel necessary but are actually premature: office space, professional branding, sophisticated software subscriptions.

The question is not whether those things would be nice to have. The question is whether they are necessary to acquire the next customer and generate the next sale. During early validation, luxury comes after sustainability. Not before.

[ON CAMERA]

Burn rate also creates an important feedback loop with your break-even analysis from yesterday.

[GRAPHIC: Side-by-side -- "Break-even: 100 cakes/month" next to "Current sales: 40 cakes/month" next to "Runway: 4 months"]

If your break-even requires one hundred sales per month and you are currently at forty, and your runway is four months, you have a very specific constraint. You need to close the gap from forty to one hundred within four months -- or find a way to dramatically reduce your fixed costs so the break-even falls to a number you can actually reach.

Seeing both numbers side by side makes the challenge concrete. It creates the urgency that theory alone never does.

[PAUSE]

Before we close, I want to share a reflection with you.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

There is an ancient principle that counsels against beginning a project without first calculating whether you have sufficient resources to complete it. The warning is not against ambition. It is against the specific kind of pride that launches boldly without pausing to count.

Burn rate and runway are, in the deepest sense, a discipline of humility. They force you to be honest about the gap between your vision and your current reality. They insist on a conversation you might prefer to avoid.

[ON CAMERA]

But the entrepreneurs who have built lasting businesses in challenging markets -- in Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, and Uzbekistan -- consistently report that the habits of financial discipline they developed early were among the most important factors in their survival. Not because those habits made the work feel smaller. But because they made the risks legible.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Managing money well, knowing your numbers, treating your resources with care -- these are not just financial strategies. They reflect a deeper orientation toward responsibility. You have been entrusted with resources, relationships, and time. Counting the cost is not a failure of faith. It is an act of faithful stewardship.

[GRAPHIC: Reflection Questions -- "Have you been avoiding looking at your financial numbers because the reality might be uncomfortable?" and "Is there a version of your business that is more financially honest and sustainable -- even if it is smaller or slower than you imagined?"]

[PAUSE]

Sit with those questions.

[GRAPHIC: Day 3 Action -- "Calculate your monthly burn rate. Divide your cash on hand by that number. That is your runway. Write it down. If it is under 3 months, we need to talk about a plan -- now."]

Do the math. I will see you tomorrow for Aijan's story -- and this one hits different. She finally ran the numbers. See you then.

## Day 5: Action -- Financial Dashboard Workshop

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day 5. Today we build your Financial Dashboard -- and I am going to be direct with you. This is the most important worksheet in the entire program.

When you finish today, you will have every critical number in one place. Your revenue per unit. Your costs. Your break-even point. Your CAC and LTV. Your burn rate and runway.

These numbers are the foundation for every pricing, spending, and growth decision you will make from now on.

[GRAPHIC: "Financial Dashboard" worksheet header]

Pull out your worksheet. Let us go section by section.

[ON CAMERA]

Section one: Revenue. Two numbers.

Your selling price per unit -- the amount a customer pays for one unit of your product or service. And your units sold per month -- how many you are currently selling in a typical month. Multiply them together. That is your monthly revenue.

[GRAPHIC: "Price x Units = Monthly Revenue"]

Write the real numbers. Not what you hope to achieve. Not last month's best day multiplied by thirty. The honest average.

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Costs. This is where you separate fixed from variable.

List every fixed cost individually. Rent or workspace. Phone and internet. Loan repayments. Any other recurring monthly costs -- equipment payments, software, insurance.

Add them all up. That is your total fixed costs per month.

Then calculate your variable cost per unit. Everything that costs you money to produce or deliver one unit -- materials, packaging, delivery, commissions. Be honest. Include the small things you usually forget.

[GRAPHIC: Step-by-step -- "Price - Variable Cost = Contribution Margin. Fixed Costs / Contribution Margin = Break-Even Units."]

Now subtract your variable cost from your selling price. That is your contribution margin. Then divide your total fixed costs by that contribution margin. The result is your break-even units per month -- the exact number of sales you need to cover all your costs.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Look at that number. Now look at your current monthly sales. Are you above or below break-even?

If you are below, that is not a failure. It is clarity. It tells you exactly how far you need to go and gives you the information you need to decide how to get there.

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Unit Economics.

What did you spend on marketing and sales last month? Include ads, materials, your own time at a fair rate, anything that went toward acquiring customers. How many new customers did that effort produce? Divide spending by new customers. That is your CAC.

Now calculate LTV. What is your average transaction value? What is your gross margin percentage -- your contribution margin divided by your price, times one hundred? How many purchases does your average customer make per year? How many years does the average customer stay with you?

[GRAPHIC: "LTV = Avg Transaction x Gross Margin % x Purchases/Year x Customer Lifespan"]

Multiply those together. That is your Customer Lifetime Value.

Now divide LTV by CAC.

[GRAPHIC: "LTV / CAC = ???" with "Target: 3:1 or higher" underneath]

That ratio should be at least three to one. If it is below three to one, you need to identify which lever to pull: reduce CAC, increase transaction value, or increase purchase frequency.

[ON CAMERA]

Section four: Burn Rate and Runway.

Write down your total monthly spending -- every som that flows out of your business. Then your total monthly revenue. Subtract revenue from spending. If the result is positive, that is your net burn rate -- the amount you are losing each month.

Now divide your cash on hand -- all money currently available to the business -- by that burn rate.

[GRAPHIC: "Cash on Hand / Net Burn Rate = Runway (months)"]

That is your runway. The number of months before the business runs out of money at its current rate.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

If that number is under six months, I want you to circle it in red. You need a plan to either reach profitability or reduce costs within that window.

If it is under three months, stop everything else in this program and focus on this. Talk to your facilitator. Talk to your mentor. We need to address this immediately.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

If your numbers look challenging, remember: these numbers are not punishing you. They are protecting you. They are giving you information you can act on -- today -- rather than discovering the truth when it is too late to do anything about it.

[ON CAMERA]

Here is your real-world activity for this week.
