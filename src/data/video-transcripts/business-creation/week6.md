## Day 1: The Mom Test

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Week 6. This is a week that changes how you think about your business. Maybe permanently.

[PAUSE]

Everything you have built so far -- your Business Model Canvas, your Customer Profile, your Value Map -- is made of assumptions. Some of those assumptions are educated guesses based on your experience in your community. Others, if we are being honest, are wishful thinking dressed up as insight.

The only way to know the difference is to go talk to real people.

[GRAPHIC: Quote card -- "Get out of the building." -- Steve Blank]

Steve Blank, one of the most influential voices in modern entrepreneurship, built his entire teaching career around four words: "Get out of the building." Stop theorizing. Stop building in isolation. Go talk to the people you think you are serving and find out if your assumptions are true.

This week, you will do exactly that. And we start with the most important concept in customer discovery: the Mom Test.

[GRAPHIC: "The Mom Test" title card -- Book cover by Rob Fitzpatrick]

[ON CAMERA]

The Mom Test gets its name from a book by Rob Fitzpatrick, and the idea is deceptively simple. Here is the problem he identified: most entrepreneurs, when they finally work up the courage to talk to potential customers, end up with conversations that feel encouraging but teach them almost nothing.

They ask, "Would you buy this?" And the customer, not wanting to be unkind, says, "Yes, I think so."

They ask, "Is this a good idea?" And the customer, seeing the entrepreneur's enthusiasm, says, "Yes, definitely."

[SERIOUS TONE]

They walk away feeling validated. But they have not learned a single fact. Not one.

[ON CAMERA]

The Mom Test is named after a simple observation: even your mother -- who loves you and wants you to succeed more than anyone in the world -- can give you useless feedback if you ask her the wrong questions.

If you ask, "Mom, do you think my business idea is good?" she will say yes. She has to. She is your mother.

But if you ask, "Mom, when you last ordered a cake for a celebration, what was the most stressful part of the whole experience?" -- she might tell you something real.

[PAUSE]

The difference is not in the person you are asking. It is in the question itself. Good questions cannot be answered politely. They require the other person to think, to remember, and to respond from their actual experience.

[ON CAMERA]

Fitzpatrick offers three rules for the Mom Test.

[GRAPHIC: "Rule 1: Talk about their life, not your idea."]

Rule one: talk about their life, not your idea. Your idea is irrelevant until you understand the customer's current reality. Ask about what they do today. How they solve the problem today. What frustrates them today.

Never pitch in a discovery interview. The moment you say, "I am building a bakery that delivers fresh cakes via Telegram," the customer's brain shifts from honest reporting to polite evaluation. You have lost access to their unfiltered experience.

[GRAPHIC: "Rule 2: Ask about specifics in the past, not generalities about the future."]

Rule two: ask about specifics in the past, not generalities or opinions about the future. "Would you use this?" is a worthless question. Nobody knows what they would hypothetically do. But "Tell me about the last time you ordered a cake for a celebration" -- that is powerful, because it accesses a real memory with real details.

[GRAPHIC: "Rule 3: Listen more than you talk."]

Rule three: listen more than you talk. Most people feel uncomfortable with silence in an interview and rush to fill it. When a customer finishes a sentence, pause. Count silently to five before you respond. I know that feels uncomfortable. Do it anyway.

[ON CAMERA]

Here is why that silence matters. Customers often reveal their most important insight in the second or third sentence after you stop talking. They are testing whether you are really listening. If you jump immediately to your next question, they conclude that you are going through a script. If you wait, they continue -- and what they say next is usually more honest than what came first.

[PAUSE]

Beyond bad questions, Fitzpatrick identifies three types of misleading data that ruin most customer interviews. He calls them the "three bad data types."

[GRAPHIC: Three columns -- "Compliments," "Fluff," "Ideas"]

First: compliments. A customer who says, "Oh, this is brilliant, I love it!" has given you nothing useful. Compliments feel like validation. But they contain zero information about whether the customer actually has the problem, how severe it is, or whether they would pay to solve it.

Second: fluff. Generic, hypothetical statements about what the customer usually does or would do. "I always try to buy local" sounds informative but tells you nothing about this specific purchase decision. Fluff is opinion masquerading as data.

Third: ideas. Customers who trust you will start generating ideas for your business. "You know what you should do? You should also offer gluten-free options." These feel like gifts. They are distractions. Your job is not to collect feature requests. Your job is to understand the customer's world deeply enough to know what to build.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, let me give you the most powerful tool in customer discovery. It is not a question. It is a follow-up.

[GRAPHIC: Large text -- "WHY?"]

When a customer says, "The ordering process was frustrating," do not move to the next question. Ask: "Why was it frustrating? What specifically made it feel that way?"

When they answer, ask "why" again. And again.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Three or four levels deep in the "why" chain, you often discover the real underlying concern. A customer who says "the delivery was unreliable" might reveal, after four "why" questions, that the real issue was this: she felt embarrassed in front of her guests when the cake arrived late to her daughter's party. That emotional reality -- public embarrassment -- is the real pain. And it demands a very different response than "we need to improve our logistics."

[ON CAMERA]

One final caution before we close today.

[GRAPHIC: Warning -- "The goal is NOT to confirm your idea. The goal is to discover the truth."]

The goal of customer discovery is not to confirm your existing idea. It is to discover the truth, whatever that turns out to be.

Some of the most important discoveries entrepreneurs make is that the problem they assumed was urgent is actually minor. Or that the customer they assumed was their primary segment is actually a poor fit. Or that the product they were building solves the wrong problem entirely.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Those discoveries feel painful in the moment. But they are priceless. They save you months or years of building something nobody wants. The entrepreneur who discovers early that their assumption was wrong is far better positioned than the one who discovers it after investing all their savings.

[GRAPHIC: Bad Questions vs. Good Questions -- "Would you buy this?" vs. "Tell me about the last time you dealt with this problem." / "Do you like this idea?" vs. "What did you try? Why did that not work?" / "Do you think this would work?" vs. "What did it cost you when it failed?"]

Study that chart. We are going to design your interview tomorrow. See you then.

## Day 2: Designing Your Interview

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 2. Yesterday you learned the Mom Test -- the principles that separate useful customer conversations from misleading ones. Today we get practical. You are going to design your interview before you conduct it.

[PAUSE]

A good customer discovery interview does not happen by accident. It is designed. Like a doctor taking a patient history, or a journalist investigating a story, the effective interviewer arrives with clear intent, a flexible structure, and the discipline to stay in the listening role rather than the presenting role.

[ON CAMERA]

But before you design your questions, you need to do something first.

[GRAPHIC: "Step 1: Write Down Your Assumptions"]

Write down every significant assumption embedded in your business model. What do you assume about your customer's problem? About how severe it is? About how they currently solve it? About what they would pay? About how they would prefer to receive the product? About what they would value most?

Every assumption is a risk. And the goal of customer discovery is to test the riskiest assumptions first -- the ones where, if you are wrong, the entire business model collapses.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, let us design the interview itself. A discovery interview typically has five phases.

[GRAPHIC: Five phases listed vertically -- "1. Warm-Up," "2. Exploration," "3. Deep-Dive," "4. Specifics," "5. Wrap-Up"]

Phase one: the warm-up. You open with permission and context. You are not selling anything. You are trying to learn.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Now, in Central Asian culture, this phase is longer and more relational than in Western business settings. You might spend the first several minutes simply connecting as people before any questions begin. And that is not wasted time. That is the mechanism through which trust is built. And trust is the precondition for honest disclosure.

[ON CAMERA]

Phase two: exploration. This is where you ask open questions about the customer's current situation and behavior. "Tell me about the last time you had to solve this problem. What happened?"

Phase three: the deep-dive. You follow the most interesting threads with "why" questions and specific follow-ups. This is where the gold lives.

Phase four: specifics. You probe for concrete data. "How often does this happen? How much does it cost you? What have you tried before?"

Phase five: wrap-up. You end with gratitude and one crucial question that many people forget to ask.

[GRAPHIC: Key question -- "Is there anything I should have asked that I didn't?"]

"Is there anything I should have asked that I didn't?" That question, right at the end, often produces the single most valuable insight of the entire conversation.

[ON CAMERA]

How many interviews do you need? Research consistently shows that five to fifteen interviews within a specific customer segment is sufficient to identify the major patterns. Fewer than five and you may be reacting to individual variation rather than true patterns. More than twenty with the same segment and you are likely hearing the same things repeated.

The goal is not quantity. It is pattern recognition. The moment you stop hearing anything genuinely new, you have enough data for this phase.

[PAUSE]

Now, let me talk about something that matters enormously in your cultural context.

[GRAPHIC: "Adapting Discovery to Central Asian Culture"]

In Kyrgyz, Kazakh, and Uzbek culture, direct questioning about problems or failures can feel rude or presumptuous -- particularly when speaking with elders or people you do not know well.

The most effective discovery conversations in Central Asian contexts often happen indirectly. They are embedded in the flow of normal conversation, structured around storytelling rather than question-and-answer, and built on the foundation of genuine hospitality.

[B-ROLL: Tea being poured into traditional bowls, two people sitting across from each other in a warm setting]

Bringing tea. Listening patiently. Asking about the person's work and family before approaching the business topic. These are not social niceties to be dispensed with quickly. They are the actual mechanism through which you earn honest answers.

[ON CAMERA]

A practical note on note-taking. Do not write during the conversation. Writing during a conversation signals that you are conducting a survey, not having a conversation, and most people respond by giving shorter, more formal answers.

Instead, hold the conversation naturally. Then spend thirty minutes immediately afterward writing down everything you can remember. Direct quotes. Stories they told. Emotions you observed. Things they seemed to downplay or avoid.

[GRAPHIC: Tip -- "The goal of your notes is not a transcript but a record of the 3-4 most important things you learned -- the surprises, the contradictions, the vivid details."]

[ON CAMERA]

After five interviews, look for patterns. What appears in every interview? What was said by multiple people in almost identical language? That repetition is a sign you are touching something real and widespread.

And here is one that people miss: what did multiple people specifically not mention, even though you expected them to? Silence around a topic you expected to be central is itself data. It may mean the problem is not as important to customers as you assumed.

[PAUSE]

Here is your takeaway for today.

[GRAPHIC: Day 2 Action Items -- "1. Write your top 5 business model assumptions, ranked by risk. 2. Write your interview script with 6-8 questions tested against the Mom Test. 3. Identify 3 specific people you will interview this week."]

You have the tools. You have the principles. Now write the script and identify the people. Tomorrow is synthesis day -- I am going to teach you the Empathy Map, the tool that turns messy interview notes into structured insight. See you then.

## Day 3: Synthesizing What You Learned -- The Empathy Map

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 3. By now, some of you have already conducted your first interviews. Others are preparing for them. Either way, today you need to understand what to do with the information once you have it.

You are going to have pages of notes. Fragments of conversation. A few surprising quotes. And probably some confusion about how all of it connects. The challenge now is synthesis -- moving from raw, messy, human data to structured insight that can drive decisions about your business.

[GRAPHIC: "The Empathy Map" title card -- four quadrants: Says, Thinks, Does, Feels]

The tool we use for this is the Empathy Map. Developed by the design firm XPLANE and used in design thinking programs worldwide, the Empathy Map gives you a framework for organizing what you heard into patterns that reveal the real shape of your customer's experience.

[ON CAMERA]

The Empathy Map has four quadrants.

[GRAPHIC: Four quadrants with descriptions]

"Says" captures direct quotes -- the specific words and phrases customers used that struck you as significant, revealing, or repeated. Direct quotes are important because they give you the actual language of your customer's world, which you can use later in your own marketing.

"Thinks" captures what you believe the customer is thinking but not saying out loud -- the beliefs, concerns, or judgments that seemed to operate beneath the surface. This quadrant requires interpretation, but it is often where the most important insights live.

"Does" captures actual behaviors -- what the customer does in response to the problem, how they currently solve it, what workarounds they have developed. Behavior is often the most honest data because people do not always know why they do what they do, but they always know what they do.

[SERIOUS TONE]

A customer who says they value quality but consistently buys the cheapest option is telling you something important through their behavior that their words obscure.

"Feels" captures the emotional undercurrent -- the anxieties, frustrations, excitements, and satisfactions that animated the conversations.

[ON CAMERA]

Building your Empathy Map works best as a physical exercise. Get a large sheet of paper or a whiteboard. Draw the four quadrants. Then go through your interview notes and write each piece of data on a separate sticky note, placing it in the appropriate quadrant.

[B-ROLL: Hands placing sticky notes on a large paper divided into four sections]

You will quickly notice patterns. Some quadrants will fill up fast while others remain sparse -- that tells you where your interviews went deep and where they stayed superficial. You may also notice that what customers "say" and what they "think" are in tension, or that what they "do" contradicts what they "feel."

These tensions are not problems to resolve. They are the most interesting and revealing data points you have.

[ON CAMERA]

[GRAPHIC: Example Empathy Map -- Food delivery entrepreneur]

Let me give you a concrete example. After interviewing five potential customers, an entrepreneur building a food delivery service fills in her Empathy Map. Under "Says" she writes: "I don't trust delivery -- food is always cold." Under "Thinks" she writes: "Probably assumes I'll just order and be disappointed again." Under "Does": "Currently picks up food herself even when she is tired." Under "Feels": "Frustrated, resigned, does not believe delivery can be different."

[PAUSE]

Look at that map. Her entire product strategy shifts. The most important thing she must prove is not speed. It is not menu variety. It is temperature guarantee and reliability. Before anything else, she must break through the resignation her customers feel about delivery as a category.

[ON CAMERA]

Once your Empathy Map is complete, the next step is to use it to update your business documents.

Return to your Business Model Canvas from Week 4. Look at each block and ask: what did I learn in my interviews that confirms, challenges, or changes what I wrote here?

[GRAPHIC: BMC blocks with question marks -- "Customer Segments: Right people?" "Channels: Right platforms?" "Value Proposition: Right message?" "Revenue Streams: Right price?"]

Maybe your Customer Segments block needs to be redefined. Your interviews may have revealed that the customers you thought were your target are actually poor fits, and a different group turned out to be far more interested.

Maybe your Channels block needs to change. Your interviews may have revealed that Instagram -- the channel you planned to use -- is not where your customers actually discover and buy products.

Your Value Proposition Canvas from Week 5 should also be revisited. Did the pains you listed match what customers actually complained about? Were the gains you planned to create the ones they actually cared about?

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

In some cases, customer discovery confirms your assumptions strongly -- and that confirmation is valuable, because it gives you evidence-based confidence rather than hope-based confidence. In other cases, discovery overturns your assumptions completely. Both outcomes are wins. The only bad outcome is conducting interviews so poorly that they produce neither confirmation nor correction.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

I want to acknowledge something here. Having your assumptions challenged by customer discovery can feel deeply discouraging. Especially if you have invested significant hope and identity in your business idea.

Many entrepreneurs react to disconfirming feedback with defensiveness: "That person did not really understand what I was offering." Or rationalization: "They were just one customer, I should not change my whole plan based on that."

These reactions are natural. But they are dangerous. The whole point of customer discovery is to find out where your assumptions are wrong before you invest money. Not after.

[PAUSE]

Being wrong in your assumptions -- and discovering it early -- is the most valuable outcome this process can produce.

[ON CAMERA]

End this week with a specific, written commitment. Based on your interviews and your Empathy Map, name one concrete change you will make to your Business Model Canvas or your Value Proposition Canvas. It does not have to be large. It could be removing a customer segment, adding a channel, or revising the language of your value proposition to match the words your customers actually used.

The practice of making evidence-based updates -- small, specific, traceable to actual customer feedback -- is the discipline that separates entrepreneurs who build what the market needs from those who build what they assumed the market needed.

[PAUSE]

Before I close, a deeper reflection.

There is a particular kind of courage required in customer discovery that is different from the courage of starting a business. The courage to start is about action -- moving forward despite uncertainty. The courage required in customer discovery is almost the opposite. It is the courage to stop, listen, and allow what you hear to change your direction.

In many cultures, including many Central Asian business contexts, changing your mind can be seen as weakness or inconsistency. The person who sets a course and stays on it is admired.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

But in early-stage business, the entrepreneur who updates their assumptions based on evidence is not weak. They are wise. They are doing what scientists do: forming hypotheses, testing them, and revising based on results. The ones who never revise are not resolute. They are simply unwilling to learn.

There is also a humility in good customer discovery that is deeply relational. When you sit across from another person and say, in essence, "I want to understand your world better than I currently do" -- you are making yourself a student. That posture of genuine curiosity and honest humility is not just a business technique. It is the foundation of any relationship built on real understanding.

[GRAPHIC: Reflection Questions -- "What would it mean for you to discover that your core business assumption was wrong? How would you respond?" and "Is there someone who will tell you the truth about your idea, even when it is uncomfortable?"]

Sit with those questions. I will see you tomorrow for the story.

## Day 5: Action -- Customer Discovery Kit + Four Hats Checkpoint #2

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day 5. Big day today. We are doing two things: walking through your Customer Discovery Kit worksheet, and then I am going to prepare you for the Four Hats Checkpoint -- the second one in this program.

Let us start with the worksheet.

[GRAPHIC: "Customer Discovery Kit" worksheet header]

[ON CAMERA]

Section one: Interview Planning. Before you talk to anyone, you need to be crystal clear on what you need to learn.

Write out your top three riskiest assumptions. These are the assumptions in your business model that, if they turned out to be wrong, would most hurt your business. Rank them by risk. Which one being wrong would be the most damaging? That is the one you test first.

Then describe who you will interview. Be specific -- what situation are they in? What experience do they need to have had to give you useful information? And plan for at least five interviews within the same customer segment.

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Your interview script. This is where you write your discovery questions -- tested against the Mom Test rules we learned on Day 1.

Start with your opening. How will you introduce yourself and the purpose of the conversation? Remember: do not mention your business idea. Frame it as learning. "I am trying to understand how people in our community handle this particular challenge."

[GRAPHIC: Six question prompts listed]

Then your questions. Question one should ask about a recent, specific experience: "Tell me about the last time you had to deal with this..." Question two explores their current solution: "What do you do today when this comes up?" Question three digs into frustrations: "What is most frustrating about how you currently handle it?" Question four asks about previous attempts: "Have you tried other ways to solve this?" Question five explores impact: "How did that affect you?"

And always plan your "why" chain follow-up. When they give an interesting answer, go deeper. Why? Why? Why? At least three times.

Close with: "Is there anything important about this topic that I did not ask about?"

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Interview notes. Record your findings within thirty minutes of finishing each interview. For each one, note who the person was -- their type, not their name -- and the three most important things you heard. Focus on surprises and direct quotes.

After three or more interviews, look for patterns. What did multiple customers say in almost identical language? What surprised you most compared to your original assumptions?

[PAUSE]

Section four: Your Empathy Map. Take your notes and organize them into the four quadrants we discussed yesterday.

[GRAPHIC: Empathy Map template -- Says, Thinks, Does, Feels]

Says: direct quotes. Thinks: what they believe but did not say. Does: their actual behaviors and workarounds. Feels: the emotional undercurrents you observed.
