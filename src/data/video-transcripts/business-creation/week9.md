## Day 1: Hypothesis-Driven Experimentation

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome back to Week 9. This is the final week of Module 3 — Validate Your Assumptions — and it is arguably the most important week of the entire course so far. Because this week, we stop talking about what you think might be true about your business, and we start finding out what actually is true.

Let me tell you something that might be uncomfortable to hear. The single most expensive mistake in entrepreneurship is treating assumptions as facts.

[PAUSE]

Think about that for a moment. Almost every catastrophic business failure — the product nobody bought, the service that solved a problem nobody actually had, the price point that felt right in the founder's head but repelled every customer who encountered it — almost every one of those failures can be traced back to an assumption that was never tested.

The entrepreneur believed something. Built a business around that belief. And discovered the truth only after spending months or years and exhausting their resources.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Hypothesis-driven experimentation exists to prevent this. Now, it is not a guarantee against failure — nothing is. But it is a system for failing faster, cheaper, and more informatively. And those three words — faster, cheaper, more informatively — are the difference between a setback you can recover from and a disaster you cannot.

[B-ROLL: Notebook with handwritten hypotheses, pen circling key words]

So what is a hypothesis? And how is it different from a plan?

A plan says: "I will do X and it will produce Y." A hypothesis says: "I believe X is true, and here is the specific evidence that would confirm or deny it."

The shift is subtle, but it is profound. A plan is emotionally committed to a particular outcome. A hypothesis is intellectually committed to finding out what is actually true.

[ON CAMERA]

Here is what I have observed over years of working with entrepreneurs. Founders who think in plans get defensive when reality fails to match them. Founders who think in hypotheses get curious.

Which one do you want to be?

[PAUSE]

Every good hypothesis has three components. Write these down.

[GRAPHIC: Three components of a hypothesis displayed as a list]

Number one: the assumption being tested. What do you believe to be true?

Number two: the measurement that will reveal whether the assumption is true. What will you observe or count?

Number three: the threshold — defined before the experiment runs — that separates a positive result from a negative one. What specific number or outcome would confirm your hypothesis, and what number or outcome would disprove it?

[ON CAMERA]

That third component — the pre-committed threshold — is the most important part, and it is the part most founders skip. Let me tell you why it matters so much.

Defining success criteria after you have seen the results is the scientific equivalent of drawing the target around the arrow after it has landed. It looks like success, but it produces no useful information.

[B-ROLL: Person looking at data on a phone, nodding thoughtfully]

What does this look like in practice? Let me give you a concrete example.

A food entrepreneur writes: "I believe that women in my neighborhood aged 25 to 45 are willing to pay 600 som for a custom celebration cake ordered with 48 hours notice. I will test this by offering pre-orders through my Instagram account for three weekends in a row. I will consider this hypothesis confirmed if I receive at least five paid pre-orders in that period at 600 som or above. I will consider it disconfirmed if I receive fewer than three."

[GRAPHIC: The hypothesis example displayed cleanly on screen]

Notice the precision. A specific price point. A specific customer segment. A specific timeframe. A specific numerical threshold. That precision is what makes it testable and actionable.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, I want to address something important. Writing hypotheses forces you to confront things you might prefer to leave vague. When you commit a hypothesis to paper, several things happen.

First, vague assumptions become specific enough to test. "I think people will like my product" becomes "I believe 20% of people who see my product at the weekend bazaar will ask about purchasing it."

Second, the act of writing forces you to confront the implicit dependencies in your assumptions. That bazaar hypothesis implies you will have a booth, which implies you know what the booth costs and have accounted for it.

Third, written hypotheses create accountability — both to yourself and to others who can hold you honest when you are tempted to reinterpret the results.

[PAUSE]

Now, not all assumptions are created equal. Some of your assumptions, if wrong, would require you to adjust a feature or a process. Those matter, but they are not urgent. Other assumptions, if wrong, would require you to fundamentally rethink the entire premise of your business.

We call those load-bearing assumptions. And they are the ones you need to test first.

[GRAPHIC: "Load-bearing assumptions: If wrong, requires full direction change"]

The economics of validation favor testing the riskiest assumptions first, not the most interesting ones and not the most comfortable ones. Start with the assumption that scares you the most — the one where, if you discovered you were wrong, everything would change.

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

I want to name the emotional resistance that this process generates. Because it is real and pretending it does not exist does not help you.

Committing in advance to a specific success threshold means committing in advance to the possibility of failure. And many founders — especially those who have invested significant emotional energy in their business idea — find this deeply uncomfortable.

There is a natural desire to leave the success criteria vague enough that any result can be interpreted favorably. I understand that desire. But that desire is the enemy of genuine learning.

The most useful hypothesis is one that you genuinely could not predict the outcome of — and that you are willing to let disprove your assumption if the evidence demands it.

[B-ROLL: Historical montage — market scenes, early businesses]

The greatest entrepreneurs in history were not the ones who were right about everything. They were the ones who found out what was wrong the fastest and adjusted the most intelligently. Amazon started as a bookstore and tested expansions into other categories one at a time. Apple nearly died before returning to product simplicity. Alibaba pivoted multiple times before finding its dominant model.

The throughline is not genius. It is the discipline of treating assumptions as hypotheses and the willingness to let the data lead.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

So here is your challenge for today. Before you do anything else this week, I want you to identify the single most important assumption underlying your business model. The one that, if it turned out to be wrong, would require you to fundamentally change direction.

Then write it as a hypothesis, using the format we just discussed: "I believe... I will test this by... I will know it is true if... I will know it is false if..."

Write it down. Commit to the threshold before you run the experiment. And get ready, because over the next two days, we are going to design and run real experiments.

That is the work of Week 9. Let's go.

## Day 2: Designing Validation Experiments

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Yesterday we talked about hypotheses — how to turn your assumptions into specific, testable predictions with defined success criteria. Today we are going to talk about the experiments themselves. How do you actually test a hypothesis when you have limited money, limited time, and a business that exists mostly in your notebook?

The answer is the minimum viable experiment.

[GRAPHIC: "Minimum Viable Experiment: The smallest, cheapest, fastest action that generates credible data on a specific hypothesis"]

The minimum viable experiment is the smallest, cheapest, fastest action that would generate credible data on a specific hypothesis. Every word in that definition matters.

Smallest — because there is a persistent temptation among ambitious founders to design experiments that are thorough, complete, and impressive. And that therefore take months to run and cost significant money.

Cheapest — because you do not have unlimited resources, and every dollar or som you spend on testing is a dollar you cannot spend on building.

Fastest — because speed of learning is your competitive advantage. A four-month experiment that tests five hypotheses simultaneously produces confounded data that is nearly impossible to interpret cleanly. A four-day experiment that tests one hypothesis with a clear success criterion produces actionable information you can use immediately.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me walk you through several types of experiments, because once you understand these, you will be able to design your own for almost any hypothesis.

[B-ROLL: Phone screen showing an Instagram post with a product photo]

The first type is the landing page test — sometimes called a smoke test. The structure is simple. You create a single page — and this can be an Instagram post, a WhatsApp message, or a basic website — that describes your product or service as if it already existed. And you invite potential customers to take a specific action. Sign up. Pre-order. Express interest. Pay a deposit.

The number of people who take that action, expressed as a percentage of those who saw the offer, is your conversion rate. And that number tells you something fundamental about whether real humans in your target market find your value proposition compelling enough to act on.

[ON CAMERA]

The second type is the concierge MVP. This is different — it tests the value of the outcome rather than the demand for a product.

In a concierge MVP, you deliver the service or result manually and personally. You do by hand what your eventual business would do at scale. A founder who wants to build a grocery delivery service does not need an app, a fleet, or a fulfillment center to test whether customers value the service. She needs to personally shop for five families and deliver the groceries.

If those families pay, return, and refer others, the hypothesis is confirmed. The question being tested is not whether the app will work. It is whether people value the outcome enough to pay for it.

[B-ROLL: Person packing food in containers, preparing for delivery]

The third type is the Wizard of Oz test. This is a close cousin of the concierge MVP. In a Wizard of Oz experiment, the customer experiences what appears to be an automated or scalable product, but behind the scenes, a human being is manually performing every function.

A customer scheduling appointments through what looks like an automated booking system does not know that a human is reading those requests and manually confirming them. The test is whether customers value and use the service — not whether the technology behind it works.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, here is where things get especially practical for those of you building businesses in Central Asia.

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Some of the most effective validation tools are the ones that use existing communication channels rather than building new infrastructure. And you already have access to all of them.

[GRAPHIC: "Central Asian Validation Tools" with four items listed]

A Telegram poll sent to a relevant group of thirty to fifty people can generate genuine demand signal in twenty-four hours for essentially no cost.

An Instagram post showing a finished product with a simple call to action — "comment if you want one this weekend" — reveals both interest and the specific appeal of particular product features based on what people respond to.

A sample table at a weekend bazaar with a clipboard asking browsers to write their phone number if they would buy at a given price point — that is a legitimate, powerful experiment that costs the price of bazaar entry and product samples.

A direct offer to ten specific people via WhatsApp is a channel test that takes five minutes.

[ON CAMERA]

The experiment does not need to be digital. It does not need to be sophisticated. It needs to produce real data.

[PAUSE]

But here is something critical I want you to understand about experiment design. You need to think carefully about what your results actually measure — and what they do not.

[SERIOUS TONE]

A hundred Instagram likes on a photo of your product does not mean a hundred people will buy it. A waiting list of fifty people does not mean fifty paying customers.

Social validation — people saying they like something, expressing interest, hitting a like button — consistently overpredicts purchase behavior by a large margin. The gap between expressed interest and actual purchase is one of the most reliable findings in consumer psychology.

The only validation that directly measures purchase behavior is a transaction. Money actually changing hands, even in a small amount.

[ON CAMERA]

So when you design your experiments, push as close to the actual transaction as you can. A pre-order with a deposit is stronger evidence than a sign-up. A sign-up is stronger evidence than a like. A like is stronger evidence than silence — but not by as much as you might hope.

[B-ROLL: Person noting responses in a notebook, tallying numbers]

The strongest validation experiments combine behavioral measurement — what people actually do — with qualitative understanding — why they did or did not do it.

If twenty people pre-ordered your product, asking five of them why they decided to order reveals the actual motivation, which may not be what you expected. If thirty people did not pre-order after seeing your offer, reaching out to five of them and asking what stopped them reveals the specific friction points. Both kinds of insight improve your next iteration in ways that pure numbers cannot.

[ON CAMERA]

One final principle for experiment design: run one experiment at a time wherever possible, and change only one variable at a time.

If you test a new price and a new product description simultaneously and sales increase, you do not know which change drove the improvement. If you raise the price and sales increase, you know. If you change the description and sales increase, you know.

This discipline of isolating variables — borrowed directly from the scientific method — produces knowledge that compounds. Each experiment builds on the last.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

All right. Here is your assignment. Go back to the hypothesis you wrote yesterday. Now design the minimum viable experiment you could run on that hypothesis — something you can complete within one week with under 5,000 som.

What type of experiment is it? A smoke test? A concierge MVP? A Telegram poll? A direct offer?

How will you measure the results? What specific number are you tracking? And remember — define success before you run the experiment, not after.

Write it all down. Because tomorrow, we are going to talk about what to do with the results when they come back. And that, I promise you, is the hardest conversation of the entire course.

## Day 3: Pivot or Persevere — Reading the Results

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

This is the hardest lesson in the course. Not the most complicated. Not the most technical. The hardest. Because today we talk about what to do when the evidence comes back.

The hardest decision in early-stage entrepreneurship is not the decision to start. It is the decision about what to do when the data tells you something you did not want to hear.

[PAUSE]

Human beings are not naturally good at processing data that contradicts their beliefs. Especially when those beliefs are attached to something they have invested their time, their identity, and their resources in. The emotional machinery that protects us from the pain of being wrong is the same machinery that prevents us from learning what the data actually says.

Reading validation results honestly requires deliberately overriding that protective machinery. And that is hard. I am not going to pretend it is not.

[ON CAMERA]

So let us talk about the pivot-or-persevere decision. What is it? It is the structured response to a completed experiment. And the key word is "structured."

A pivot is not abandoning your business. A pivot is a structured course correction — a change in one or more elements of your business model based on what the data revealed, while preserving what has been learned.

And persevering is equally legitimate. If the data confirms your hypothesis, or if results are promising but not yet conclusive, continuing on your current trajectory with renewed confidence is a valid and responsible choice.

[GRAPHIC: "Pivot-or-Persevere Decision: A deliberate choice based on evidence, not an emotional reaction to a feeling"]

The key word in both cases is "deliberate." The decision is made with explicit reference to the evidence. Not emotionally. Not in a panic. Not at 2 AM when you are exhausted and doubting everything. Deliberately, with the data in front of you.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me walk you through the types of pivots, because knowing the vocabulary helps clarify your options.

A zoom-in pivot happens when you discover that one specific feature or use case of your broader product is generating disproportionate value — and you narrow your focus to make that the whole product.

A zoom-out pivot is the inverse. What you thought was the entire product turns out to be just one feature of a larger solution customers actually need.

A customer segment pivot occurs when you discover that your product solves the problem brilliantly — but not for the customers you targeted. A different segment values it far more.

A platform pivot happens when what you built as a product turns out to be more valuable as a platform — or vice versa.

[GRAPHIC: Four pivot types displayed with brief definitions]

Each of these is a legitimate strategic move. None of them is failure. All of them require data to justify and courage to execute.

[ON CAMERA]

Now let me talk about the emotional difficulty. Because I know some of you are going to need to hear this, possibly this very week.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

When you have spent months thinking about a business, telling friends and family about it, organizing your life around it — changing direction feels like failure. It feels like admitting you were wrong. It activates shame.

And for entrepreneurs in communities where public ambition is culturally visible — where you told your neighborhood you were starting a business and they have been watching — the social dimension of pivoting adds another layer.

I want you to hear me clearly: pivoting is not failure. Pivoting is learning applied. The most successful companies in the world are companies that pivoted. The founders who failed are the ones who refused to pivot when the evidence demanded it, because their ego could not absorb the cost of admitting they needed to change.

[PAUSE]

Here is a useful heuristic for the pivot-or-persevere decision. Ask yourself: if a knowledgeable, respected friend looked at my data with no emotional stake in the outcome, what would they advise?

This imaginary advisor is not looking for reasons to quit. She is looking at the evidence and asking whether it points toward your current direction being viable.

[ON CAMERA]

If your experiment produced three paying customers out of a hundred potential customers approached, and you had defined success as five paying customers, your steel-man friend would probably say: the signal is weak but not zero. Before pivoting, understand why the other ninety-seven did not buy. Before persevering, understand whether three is a meaningful signal or just statistical noise.

[B-ROLL: Notebook with data, person reviewing numbers carefully]

The discipline of documenting your pivot-or-persevere decisions is valuable beyond the immediate decision. Maintaining a written record of what you tested, what you found, what you decided, and why creates organizational memory. Founders who make implicit decisions without documentation often find themselves relitigating the same questions months later.

[ON CAMERA]

When the data clearly says stop — when conversion is near zero, when every customer interaction reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of the problem, when the unit economics remain broken regardless of the variables you adjust — the most important skill is the ability to distinguish between a bad business model and a bad execution of a good business model.

The first requires a pivot. The second requires a process improvement. And the difference matters enormously, because they call for opposite responses.

[SERIOUS TONE]

A business that has the right model but is executing it poorly should be improved, not pivoted. A business that is executing well but has the wrong model should be pivoted, not optimized. Getting this distinction right is one of the most consequential judgments an entrepreneur makes.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, I want to give you a deeper perspective on all of this. Because for many of you, this is not just a strategic question. It is a spiritual one.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

There is a long tradition in wisdom literature and human experience of honoring the person who tests their convictions against reality rather than simply asserting them. Ancient agricultural cultures knew that the farmer who tested new seeds in small plots before replanting the entire field survived droughts that destroyed those who planted everything on faith alone.

For entrepreneurs of faith in Central Asia, there is sometimes a tension between the conviction that an idea is God-given and the discipline of testing it against evidence. But I want to suggest that these are not opposites.

Stewardship — responsible management of the resources entrusted to us — requires the humility to find out whether our plans are as sound as our intentions. Conviction without testing is not faith. It is presumption. The willingness to test your assumptions, follow the evidence, and adjust your direction is not a sign of wavering belief. It is a sign of faithfulness to the responsibility of good stewardship.

[PAUSE]

The pivot-or-persevere decision, at its deepest level, asks a spiritual as well as strategic question: am I attached to my plan, or am I attached to the outcome I am trying to create?

The entrepreneur attached to the plan will hold on past the point the evidence demands release. The entrepreneur attached to the outcome will pivot as many times as necessary to find the path that actually leads there.

[ON CAMERA]

Remember: validation is not a one-time event. The most successful businesses run validation experiments continuously, at every stage of growth. The questions change, but the discipline remains constant. You never reach a point where assumptions can safely be treated as facts.

The entrepreneurs in Central Asia who have built durable businesses in difficult economic conditions — through currency volatility, supply chain disruption, political uncertainty — share one observable pattern. They stay close to their customers with the specific intention of discovering when their assumptions have become outdated. They do not wait for a crisis to reveal what has changed. They find out before the crisis arrives.

That is what validation, practiced persistently, actually buys you. Not certainty. But early warning.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Tomorrow we will hear Aijan's story — how she designed and ran her first real validation experiment and what the data told her. I think you will recognize some of your own experience in her journey.

See you then.

## Day 5: Validation Designer Worksheet + Four Hats Checkpoint #3

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day 5. This is a big day — we have got two things to cover. First, we are walking through your Validation Designer worksheet, which is the tool that brings together everything we have learned this week: hypotheses, experiments, and pivot-or-persevere decisions, all in one place. [GRAPHIC: "Validation Designer Worksheet" title card]

How you will measure it. What specific, observable action or outcome will you count? Pre-orders? Conversion rate? Repeat purchases? Referrals?

And the success threshold — defined before the experiment runs. "I will consider this confirmed if [specific number or outcome] happens within [timeframe]. I will consider it disconfirmed if [specific threshold] is not met."

There is also a checkbox: "Is this a load-bearing assumption?" Remember, a load-bearing assumption is one where being wrong would require a full pivot. Check that box honestly.

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Experiments. For each hypothesis, you design the minimum viable experiment. What exactly will you do? How many people will you approach? What channel will you use? What counts as a "yes"?

You also record the experiment type — is it an Instagram pre-order test, a bazaar sample table, a Telegram poll, a direct WhatsApp offer, a concierge MVP?

And you estimate the duration and cost. Most experiments should be under seven days and under 5,000 som. If your experiment requires more than that, you probably have not found the minimum viable version yet.

[B-ROLL: Person writing in worksheet, filling in experiment details]

After you run the experiment — not before — you fill in the quantitative results. The specific numbers: how many people saw the offer, how many responded, how many converted, what the conversion rate was.

Then the qualitative results. What did people say? What questions did they ask? What surprised you?

And finally, the checkbox: hypothesis confirmed?

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Pivot or Persevere Decision. This is the most important section. After completing your experiments, you make a deliberate, documented decision.

Summarize all the evidence — including the disappointing parts. What does the evidence confirm? What does it challenge?

Then make the call. Pivot, persevere, or partial pivot — specify which element is changing.

Write your rationale. Reference specific data points. If you are persevering despite weak results, explain your reasoning. If you are pivoting, name what specifically is changing and which type of pivot it is — zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, or platform.

And regardless of whether you pivot or persevere, write your next hypothesis. The cycle never stops.

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Now — Four Hats Checkpoint number three. This is your third checkpoint, and it is focused specifically on your validation data.

[GRAPHIC: Four Hats icons — White, Black, Yellow, Gold]

Here is how it works. You are going to present what your validation experiments showed. Share your data — both the numbers and the qualitative reactions — and explain what you learned.

Then your group puts on the four hats:

White Hat asks: what are the specific numbers and facts from the experiments? How many people were approached? How many responded? What was the conversion rate? What was the precise outcome relative to the pre-committed success threshold?

[PAUSE]

Black Hat asks: where is the data weakest? What critical assumptions remain untested? Where might you be cherry-picking positive signals and discounting negative ones? What are the biggest risks the validation process has not addressed?

[ON CAMERA]

Yellow Hat asks: what results are most encouraging? Where is the strongest product-market fit signal? Which customer reactions suggest genuine, unprompted enthusiasm?

And Gold Hat asks: based on the data, what pivot could strengthen the business model? Is there an alternative customer segment, a different price point, a different channel that the data hints at — even indirectly? What is the one change that would most improve the odds of success?

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

The big question your group is answering today: should this entrepreneur pivot or persevere? Look at the full dataset, including the inconvenient parts. What does the evidence actually say, independent of what the founder hopes it says?

[ON CAMERA]

This is challenging work. It requires vulnerability to share data that might not look great, and it requires honesty from your group to give real feedback. But this is where the Four Hats process earns its value — structured feedback from people who understand the frameworks but are not emotionally attached to your specific outcome.

Take your time with this. Be thorough. Be honest. And remember — a well-reasoned pivot is not a failure. It is the smartest thing an entrepreneur can do.
