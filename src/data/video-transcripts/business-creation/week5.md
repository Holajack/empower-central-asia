## Day 1: Customer Jobs -- What Is Your Customer Trying to Accomplish?

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome back to Week 5. Last week you built your Business Model Canvas -- you mapped your entire business as a system of nine interconnected blocks. This week, we go deeper into the single most important block on that canvas: your Value Proposition.

[PAUSE]

Here is the quote that should guide everything you do this week. Uri Levine, the founder of Waze -- the navigation app used by hundreds of millions of people -- said this: "Fall in love with the problem, not the solution."

[GRAPHIC: Quote card -- "Fall in love with the problem, not the solution." -- Uri Levine]

That sounds simple. But most entrepreneurs do the exact opposite. They fall in love with their product. They fall in love with their recipe, their design, their technique. And then they spend months -- sometimes years -- trying to convince customers to love it too.

This week, we flip that. We start with the customer. We start with what they are trying to accomplish in their life. And only after we deeply understand that do we design our product to fit their world -- rather than asking them to fit ours.

[PAUSE]

So let us begin with a concept that Alexander Osterwalder -- the same person who created the Business Model Canvas -- calls "customer jobs."

[GRAPHIC: "Customer Jobs" title card with three icons -- wrench (functional), people (social), heart (emotional)]

Every customer who walks through your door, sends you a message, or stops at your market stall is trying to get something done. They have a task, a need, a problem. Something brought them to you. That something is the "job."

Now, here is what is critical to understand: a job is not the same as a product.

[B-ROLL: Woman browsing a bakery display case, looking at celebration cakes]

Nobody wakes up in the morning wanting to buy a cake. They wake up wanting to celebrate their daughter's birthday and impress their guests without the stress of cooking for thirty people. The cake is the tool. The job is what the tool accomplishes.

When you understand that distinction, everything about how you design your business changes.

[ON CAMERA]

Customer jobs fall into three categories, and you need to understand all three.

[GRAPHIC: Three-column layout -- "Functional Jobs," "Social Jobs," "Emotional Jobs"]

The first is functional jobs -- the practical, task-oriented things your customer needs to accomplish. A teacher needs lesson plans prepared before class. A mother needs dinner on the table by seven. A student needs to pass the entrance exam. These are concrete. They are measurable. They are the surface-level reason someone comes to you.

But underneath every functional job is a social job -- the way the customer wants to be perceived by others.

[SERIOUS TONE]

The teacher does not just want lesson plans. She wants to be seen as professional and respected by her principal and her students. The mother does not just want dinner done. She wants to be seen as someone who holds the family together -- someone who is competent even while working full time. The student does not just want to pass the exam. He wants to be the person in his family who finally went to university.

Social jobs are incredibly powerful motivators. But here is the thing -- customers rarely say them out loud. A customer does not walk up to you and say, "I want people to think I am successful." They say, "I need reliable delivery by Friday." But what they mean is, "My reputation depends on this going right."

[ON CAMERA]

Let me give you a specific example that puts all three types together.

[GRAPHIC: Example card -- "Kyrgyz Primary School Teacher"]

Consider a primary school teacher in Kyrgyzstan who needs tutoring support for students who are falling behind. Her functional job is straightforward: find someone who can help her students improve their reading and math before the end-of-year exam.

But her social job is equally real: she wants to be seen by the school director as a teacher who is proactive, who cares, who takes initiative.

And her emotional job -- the third category -- is this: she wants to feel that she is doing right by her students. She wants relief from the guilt of knowing that some children are slipping through the cracks and she cannot reach them all on her own.

[PAUSE]

Now, think about how this changes the way a tutor markets themselves.

[B-ROLL: Tutor working with a child at a desk]

The tutor who advertises "certified math instruction with structured curriculum" is speaking to the functional job. Fine.

But the tutor who says "your child will walk into that exam with confidence" -- that tutor is speaking to the emotional job. Both statements are true. But the second one is what actually moves a mother to pick up the phone.

[ON CAMERA]

Jobs also vary in importance. Some jobs are critical -- the customer absolutely must get them done and will pay significantly to solve them. Other jobs are minor inconveniences that the customer has learned to live with. When you are mapping your customer's jobs, resist the temptation to list only the obvious, surface-level functional jobs. Push deeper. Ask what social situation they are navigating. Ask what emotional state they are carrying.

And here is one more important nuance. Customers often have multiple jobs they are trying to accomplish simultaneously -- and those jobs can conflict with each other.

[GRAPHIC: Example -- "The Mother's Conflicting Jobs"]

The mother wants dinner done quickly -- that is functional. But she also wants it to be the healthy, homemade kind -- that is social and emotional. And she does not want to spend too much money -- that is functional again. These jobs are in tension.

The best value propositions acknowledge those tensions and offer a resolution. Not fast food, and not gourmet cooking -- but something that honors the truth of what that mother is actually navigating.

[PAUSE]

So here is your assignment for today. For your primary customer segment -- the person you identified in your Business Model Canvas -- write down every job you think they are trying to accomplish. Do not filter. Do not evaluate yet. List functional jobs, social jobs, and emotional jobs. List the ones you are confident about and the ones you are guessing about.

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Because next week, when you go out and interview real customers, you will have a framework that sharpens your listening. You will not just hear what they say -- you will hear what lies underneath what they say. That is what separates average entrepreneurs from great ones.

[GRAPHIC: Day 1 Summary -- "Customer Jobs: Functional (what they do), Social (how they want to be seen), Emotional (how they want to feel)"]

I will see you tomorrow, when we talk about what frustrates your customer and what delights them -- pains and gains. See you then.

## Day 2: Pains and Gains -- What Frustrates Them and What Delights Them?

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 2 of Week 5. Yesterday we identified the jobs your customer is trying to accomplish -- the functional, social, and emotional tasks that bring them to you. Today we go to the next level. We ask: what does it actually feel like for your customer to try to get those jobs done?

That is where pains and gains come in.

[GRAPHIC: "Pains & Gains" title card with a dividing line -- frustrated face on left, delighted face on right]

Pains are the negative experiences, the frustrations, and the risks your customer encounters as they try to get their job done. Gains are the outcomes and benefits they desire -- not just what they minimally need, but what would genuinely delight them.

Now, it is tempting to think of pains and gains as simple opposites -- that a gain is just the removal of a pain. This is a mistake, and I want you to really understand why.

[PAUSE]

A customer who finds that delivery is unreliable has a pain. Reliable delivery does not just remove that pain -- it creates a gain: peace of mind, the ability to plan confidently, the relief of not having to follow up. But the customer might also want something beyond reliability. Maybe they want to be notified automatically when their order is on the way. Maybe they want the delivery person to call ahead. Maybe they want a small apology note if there is any delay.

[B-ROLL: Phone notification showing "Your order is 15 minutes away"]

Those gains go beyond the absence of pain. They represent a positive experience the customer would not have thought to demand, but would love to receive.

[ON CAMERA]

Let us start with pains. Pains fall into three categories.

[GRAPHIC: Three categories of pain -- "Undesired Outcomes," "Obstacles," "Risks"]

First: undesired outcomes. These are the things that go wrong in the current process. The order arrives wrong. The quality is inconsistent. The service takes too long.

Second: obstacles. These are the things that prevent your customer from even starting the job. The process is too complicated. They do not know where to go. They cannot afford it. They lack the right contacts.

Third: risks. These are the feared negative consequences. What happens if this goes badly? What is at stake if I choose the wrong supplier? If I trust the wrong person? If I invest in the wrong product?

[SERIOUS TONE]

Now here is where it gets strategic. Severity matters enormously when you are evaluating pains.

[GRAPHIC: Pain severity scale -- 1 (minor inconvenience) to 5 (extreme daily frustration)]

A mild inconvenience and a paralyzing obstacle are both pains, but they require very different responses. When you are mapping customer pains, force yourself to rank them by severity. Which pains does your customer experience daily? Which pains have they tried to solve and failed? Which pains have they simply accepted because no good solution exists?

The pains that are most frequent, most severe, and most unsolved -- those are the ones where the most value awaits whoever can address them.

[ON CAMERA]

Now let us talk about gains. Gains also vary in importance, and the distinctions here are strategically critical.

[GRAPHIC: Four types of gains -- "Required," "Expected," "Desired," "Unexpected"]

Some gains are required -- the customer expects them as a baseline and will not even consider your offer without them. A restaurant must serve food that is edible. A taxi must get you to your destination. These are not competitive advantages. They are basic requirements.

Beyond that are expected gains -- things the customer assumes come with the offer. The food should also be warm. The taxi should have working seatbelts.

Then come desired gains -- benefits the customer wants and would value, but has not articulated as a requirement. Maybe they would love a restaurant that texts them when their table is ready.

And finally, unexpected gains -- things the customer never imagined asking for, but that delight them when they experience them.

[B-ROLL: Restaurant staff bringing a small free dessert to a table with a birthday candle]

The restaurant that remembers your birthday and gives you a free dessert creates an unexpected gain. That is the kind of moment that turns a customer into an advocate.

[ON CAMERA]

Here is why this distinction matters strategically. Competing on required and expected gains is exhausting. You are just meeting the baseline, and your competitors can match you every time.

The businesses that create deep customer loyalty -- the ones people talk about, the ones people recommend to their friends -- often do so by delivering desired or unexpected gains. The pleasant surprises. The moments that make a customer feel seen and valued.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

In Central Asian markets, where trust and relationship are foundational to commerce, the unexpected gain is often relational. The supplier who calls to check in without being asked. The tutor who sends a note of encouragement on exam day. The shopkeeper who remembers a regular customer's preferences without being reminded. These are not expensive gestures. But they are powerful ones.

[ON CAMERA]

Now, I want to warn you about a common trap.

[GRAPHIC: Warning -- "Be Specific, Not Vague"]

When you are mapping pains and gains, avoid writing vague, generic entries. "Bad quality" is not a pain -- it is a category. But this is a pain: "The cake arrived crushed because it was not packaged properly for transport, and the frosting was ruined thirty minutes before the party started." That level of specificity tells you exactly what to fix.

Same with gains. "They want it to be delicious" is obvious. It tells you nothing. But "they want to hear their guests say it was the best cake they have ever eaten and ask where it came from" -- that is a gain you can actually design for.

[PAUSE]

Specificity transforms your customer map from a theoretical exercise into actionable design guidance. Do not settle for vague entries. Push yourself until every pain and every gain reads like a real scene from a real person's life.

[ON CAMERA]

One final point. Pains and gains are not static. They change as your customer's situation changes. A new mother has different pains than she did as a student. A business owner in their third year has different concerns than one in their first month.

The best entrepreneurs stay in continuous conversation with their customers precisely because pains and gains evolve. The value proposition that was a perfect fit two years ago may need significant updating today.

[GRAPHIC: Day 2 Action Item -- "Rank your customer's top 3 pains by severity (1-5 scale). Rank their top 3 gains by importance. Only pains ranked 4 or 5 deserve a place in your value proposition."]

Today, I want you to list out your customer's pains and their gains. Then rank them. The top three pains by severity, the top three gains by importance. Those rankings will drive the decisions you make tomorrow when we build your Value Map.

See you then.

## Day 3: The Value Map -- How Your Product Relieves Pains and Creates Gains

**Video Length:** ~15 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome to Day 3. This is where everything comes together.

Over the past two days you have built a rich picture of your customer -- their jobs, their pains, and their gains. Today we build the other side of the Value Proposition Canvas: the Value Map.

[GRAPHIC: Value Proposition Canvas diagram -- Customer Profile on the right (circle with Jobs, Pains, Gains), Value Map on the left (square with Products & Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators)]

The Value Map has three elements that mirror the Customer Profile. Your Products and Services answer the question: what do you offer? Your Pain Relievers answer: how specifically does your offer address your customer's top pains? And your Gain Creators answer: how specifically does your offer create the outcomes and benefits your customer desires?

[PAUSE]

The most important word in the Value Map is "specifically."

[SERIOUS TONE]

It is not enough to say "our product solves customer problems." Every business on earth claims this. The power of the Value Map is that it forces you to draw explicit, traceable lines from each element of your offer to each specific pain or gain in your customer's profile.

[GRAPHIC: Arrows connecting specific pains to specific pain relievers -- with examples]

If you cannot draw that line clearly, the connection probably does not exist. And your customer will not feel it either.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me walk you through what strong pain relievers look like.

Pain relievers describe exactly how your products and services eliminate or reduce specific customer pains. They might eliminate negative outcomes: "Our packaging ensures the cake arrives intact regardless of road conditions."

[B-ROLL: Hands carefully placing a cake into a sturdy, custom-fitted delivery box]

They might remove obstacles: "You can place your order via Telegram without needing to call, visit, or negotiate in person."

They might reduce risk: "We offer a money-back guarantee if the order is not ready on time."

[ON CAMERA]

Now here is the critical principle: not every pain needs to be addressed in your value proposition.

[GRAPHIC: Warning callout -- "Great value propositions address the most severe pains exceptionally well, rather than addressing every possible pain poorly."]

This is one of the most common traps entrepreneurs fall into. They list twenty pain relievers and twenty gain creators to seem thorough. But in practice, their product addresses none of them exceptionally well.

Focus. Choose the three most important pains in your customer profile and design your product to relieve those three with excellence. Depth beats breadth in value proposition design. Every time.

[ON CAMERA]

Now let us talk about gain creators. These describe how your products and services create specific customer gains.

They might produce outcomes the customer needs: "Your guests will be impressed."

They might go further than expected: "We photograph your cake upon delivery so you have a memory of the moment."

[B-ROLL: Baker taking a photo of a finished cake on a table with nice lighting]

They might create social gains: "Every order includes the baker's personal card, which customers can show their guests."

Or they might reduce effort: "You place one order and we handle everything, including delivery time coordination, so you have one less thing to think about on the day of the event."

[ON CAMERA]

The goal of all of this -- the Customer Profile and the Value Map together -- is what Osterwalder calls "fit."

[GRAPHIC: "FIT" in large letters -- "When your Pain Relievers and Gain Creators align closely with the most important Pains and Gains in your Customer Profile"]

Fit is not just satisfaction. It is the feeling a customer gets when they encounter a product or service that seems to understand them completely. That seems designed specifically for their life, their situation, their constraints.

When customers experience fit, they do not just buy. They return. They refer. They become advocates.

[PAUSE]

Fit comes in stages, and it is important to know where you are.

[GRAPHIC: Three stages of fit -- "1. Problem-Solution Fit" (highlighted), "2. Product-Market Fit," "3. Business Model Fit"]

The first stage is Problem-Solution Fit: you have identified real, important customer jobs, pains, and gains, and you have designed a product that genuinely addresses them. This is what you are working toward right now, in Weeks 4 through 6 of this program.

The second stage is Product-Market Fit: you have evidence from real customers that your solution is actually working -- people are buying, using, and returning.

The third stage is Business Model Fit: your entire Business Model Canvas holds together as a sustainable system.

[ON CAMERA]

Let me give you one more powerful technique before we close today.

Some designers call it "progressive revelation." Rather than listing every feature and benefit of your product at once, ask yourself this question:

[GRAPHIC: Key question -- "Which single pain or gain is so important that addressing it alone would be enough to make a customer try my offer?"]

Start there. Build your core value around the one thing that matters most. Once you have proven that works, add the next most important pain reliever or gain creator.

This approach keeps you disciplined. It prevents the common mistake of building an overcomplicated offer that tries to do everything and ends up doing nothing well.

[PAUSE]

Now, go back to your Business Model Canvas from Week 4. Look at your Value Proposition block. Now that you have built a Customer Profile and a Value Map, does that statement still capture what you know? Does it speak to functional jobs, social jobs, and emotional jobs? Does it name specific pains? Does it articulate gains -- including the unexpected gains that will set you apart?

Revise it. Carry that revision forward into Week 6, when you will test it against the responses of real customers.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Before I let you go, I want to share a reflection with you.

In many entrepreneurship programs, students are taught to present, to pitch, to persuade. And those are real skills. But there is an earlier, deeper skill that determines whether those presentations are built on solid ground: the ability to genuinely listen to another person's experience without immediately jumping to solutions.

[PAUSE]

In Central Asian cultures, there is a tradition of extended conversation before business. The tea shared before the negotiation. The family news exchanged before the price is discussed. This tradition contains wisdom that the modern startup world has been slowly rediscovering through the language of "customer discovery."

Real understanding of another person's situation takes time. It requires patience with ambiguity. It requires resisting the urge to fill silence with your own thoughts.

The best entrepreneurs in any culture are often unusually good listeners. They are curious about other people's experiences in a way that feels genuine, not transactional.

[ON CAMERA]

So when you sit down with a potential customer -- this week, next week, whenever that conversation happens -- your only goal is to understand their world. Not to convince them of anything. Not to fix anything. Just to understand. That posture -- humble, curious, genuinely interested -- is a character quality as much as a business skill.

[GRAPHIC: Reflection Questions -- "How comfortable are you with sitting in silence after a customer finishes speaking? What is one assumption about your customer's pains that you have never actually verified?"]

Sit with those questions today. We will need that posture of listening very soon.

[GRAPHIC: Day 3 Summary -- "Value Map = Products + Pain Relievers + Gain Creators. Draw direct lines from each to specific customer pains and gains. Focus on the top 3. Depth beats breadth."]

I will see you tomorrow for Aijan's story. You are going to hear about what happened when she finally asked her customers what they actually wanted. See you then.

## Day 5: Action -- Value Proposition Canvas Workshop

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day 5. Action day. Today you are going to build your complete Value Proposition Canvas -- and by the end of this session, you will have a document that connects every part of your product to a specific need in your customer's life.

[GRAPHIC: Value Proposition Canvas template -- two sides: Customer Profile (circle) and Value Map (square)]

Pull out your worksheet. You should have it open in front of you. We are going to work through it section by section.

[PAUSE]

Start with the Customer Profile -- the right side of the canvas. You have three sections here.

[ON CAMERA]

Section one: Jobs. You did this work on Day 1. Write out your customer's functional jobs -- the practical tasks they need to accomplish. Then their social jobs -- how they want to be seen by others. Then their emotional jobs -- how they want to feel.

[GRAPHIC: Example filled in -- Functional: "Feed 30 guests at a birthday party." Social: "Be seen as a generous, capable host." Emotional: "Feel the joy of seeing her child's face light up."]

Do not rush this. Think about a specific person. Give them a face in your mind. What is their day like? What pressure are they under? What are they trying to prove to the people around them?

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Pains. List every frustration, obstacle, and risk your customer faces when trying to get those jobs done. Be specific -- not "poor quality" but exactly what goes wrong. The cake arrived crushed. The baker changed the price three times. The delivery was forty-five minutes late to a party that had already started.

[GRAPHIC: Pain severity scale reminder -- "Rank each pain 1-5. Only 4s and 5s belong in your value proposition."]

Then rank each pain on a severity scale of one to five. Five is extreme -- a daily frustration that causes real damage. One is a minor inconvenience they barely notice. Only the pains you rank at four or five deserve a prominent place in your value proposition. The others are good to know, but they should not drive your design.

Do not forget to list obstacles -- things that prevent customers from even getting started -- and risks -- the things they fear will go wrong.

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Gains. List the outcomes and benefits your customer desires. Start with required gains -- the baseline expectations they will not compromise on. Then desired gains -- the things that would make them genuinely happy. And finally, unexpected gains -- the delightful surprises they have never received from anyone in your category.

[GRAPHIC: Gains example -- Required: "The cake must taste good and arrive on time." Desired: "Seeing a photo of the cake before delivery." Unexpected: "A handwritten card from the baker."]

[PAUSE]

Now flip to the Value Map -- the left side of the canvas.

[ON CAMERA]

First, describe your core product or service. What exactly does a customer receive when they buy from you? Be concrete.

Then, Pain Relievers. For each of your top three pains -- the ones you ranked four or five -- write exactly how your product or service addresses that pain. Be specific. Not "better quality" but "every cake is photographed before delivery so the customer sees exactly what they are getting."

Then, Gain Creators. How does your offer produce the required gains, the desired gains, and the unexpected gains you listed?

[GRAPHIC: Drawing arrows from Pain Relievers to specific Pains, and from Gain Creators to specific Gains]

The magic is in the arrows. Can you draw a direct line from each feature of your product to a specific pain or gain? If the arrow does not connect to anything on the customer side, that feature may not be as important as you think.

[ON CAMERA]

Finally, the Fit Assessment. This is the honest conversation with yourself.

Where do you have the strongest fit? Where does your offer most directly and powerfully address a real customer pain or gain?

And where do you have the biggest gap? Where is your offer falling short of what the customer actually needs?

[SERIOUS TONE]

Be honest about the gaps. Every first draft has them. Identifying them is the first step to fixing them. And fixing them is what Weeks 6 through 8 are all about.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Now, here is your real-world activity for this week, and this one is important.

[GRAPHIC: Real-World Activity -- "Interview 3 Potential Customers Using the Jobs-Pains-Gains Framework"]

Choose three people who represent your target customer. Ask each one: "What are you trying to get done in this area of your life?" Then: "What frustrates you most about the current way of doing it?" Then: "What would a perfect solution look like for you?"

Do not pitch your product. Only listen. Take written notes. After all three interviews, compare what you heard to your Value Map. Were your assumptions correct? What surprised you?

[ON CAMERA]
