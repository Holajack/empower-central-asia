## Day 1: Where Opportunities Hide

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Week Three. This is the final week of Module One — "Think Like an Entrepreneur" — and it is the week where everything comes together.

In Week One, you learned to question your assumptions and test before you build. In Week Two, you learned to protect your time and focus on what matters. This week, we answer the question that everything else has been building toward: what business should you actually build?

Not all business ideas are created equal. Some ideas are solutions looking for a problem. Others are genuine opportunities — real unmet needs in real markets that a specific entrepreneur is positioned to address. This week, you are going to learn how to find the difference.

[PAUSE]

Let us start by challenging a myth. The popular mythology of entrepreneurship tells the story of a lone genius who invents something the world has never seen. The reality is far more ordinary — and far more encouraging.

The vast majority of successful businesses were built on a clear-eyed observation that a specific group of people has a problem, and the recognition that the person observing it might be in a position to solve it.

[B-ROLL: Quick examples — James Dyson holding a vacuum cleaner, Sara Blakely, then Central Asian examples — a delivery driver on a motorcycle in Bishkek, someone at a market stall]

[ON CAMERA]

James Dyson did not invent the vacuum cleaner. He noticed that existing ones had a specific frustrating flaw — bags that clogged and lost suction — and spent years building a better one. Sara Blakely did not invent undergarments. She noticed that nothing on the market solved a specific problem she had with her own clothes.

Most great businesses begin not with invention but with noticing.

[PAUSE]

Problems are the richest source of opportunity, and they are everywhere.

Delivery services in Bishkek have grown explosively not because delivery is a novel idea, but because enough people living in high-rise apartments noticed that getting fresh food required a trip on multiple minibuses, and someone noticed their frustration acutely enough to do something about it.

Agricultural modernization businesses in the Fergana Valley exist because experienced farmers noticed their parents and neighbors were spending enormous labor on tasks that technology could handle more reliably.

Every time you hear someone say "I wish there was a better way to do this," you are listening to a possible business.

[PAUSE]

Trends are the second major source of opportunity. A trend is a sustained directional shift in the behavior, values, or capabilities of a population.

[GRAPHIC: Trends creating opportunity in Central Asia:
- Rapid smartphone growth across Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan → mobile-first businesses
- Growing middle class in Almaty and Bishkek → demand for quality over quantity
- Increasing women's participation in formal economy → childcare, professional clothing, time-saving services
- Rising tourism → hospitality, guided experiences, craft goods]

[ON CAMERA]

The entrepreneur who spots a trend early and builds for where the market is heading — not where it is now — has a significant advantage.

[PAUSE]

Gaps are the third category — spaces where customer need exists but supply does not. And this is where a tool called PEST analysis comes in.

PEST stands for Political, Economic, Social, and Technological — the four categories of external forces that shape what is possible, necessary, or profitable in a given market.

[GRAPHIC: PEST Framework:
P — Political: regulations, government programs, licensing
E — Economic: income levels, costs, spending patterns
S — Social: cultural shifts, demographics, lifestyle changes
T — Technological: new tools, smartphone penetration, platforms]

[ON CAMERA]

A political analysis might reveal which government programs are creating new demand, or what regulations make certain businesses difficult — or newly possible — to start. An economic analysis shows what income levels are growing, what costs are falling, what spending categories are increasing. Social analysis tracks cultural shifts. Technological analysis identifies new tools and platforms.

The key is to apply PEST to a specific community — a town, a district, a neighborhood — rather than to a whole country. A PEST analysis of Osh reveals different patterns than one of Nur-Sultan. The specificity matters.

[PAUSE]

Let me tell you about a real example.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "A young entrepreneur in Tashkent noticed that women in her neighborhood frequently had trouble finding modest professional clothing that fit well and didn't require expensive alterations. The tailors who could do the work had no online presence and were hard to find. She built a simple directory connecting customers with trusted local tailors — no sewing required, just problem recognition and organization. Within three months she had 40 tailors listed and was earning a referral fee on every connection."]

[ON CAMERA]

She did not make anything. She connected people. She noticed a gap and built a bridge across it. That is opportunity identification at its purest.

[PAUSE]

Now, Instagram has become one of the most visible channels for opportunity identification in Central Asia. But I want you to be careful with what you see there. The businesses visible on Instagram are not necessarily profitable. The categories that look popular may be more competitive than they appear.

When you see twenty homemade food businesses on Instagram in your city, the correct question is not "should I start another one?" The correct question is: "What do the customers of those businesses need that none of those twenty are providing?"

Gaps exist even inside crowded categories, and they are often more profitable than the categories themselves.

[PAUSE]

One of the most powerful opportunity-identification practices is also the simplest: keep a problem journal.

[GRAPHIC: Problem Journal instructions:
- For 1 week, carry a notebook or use your phone
- Write down every frustration, inefficiency, and unmet need you observe
- Your own AND others'
- Do not filter. Do not evaluate. Just capture.
- End of week: Which problems are BIG enough, FREQUENT enough, and PAINFUL enough that people would pay to have them solved?]

[ON CAMERA]

The problems that meet all three criteria — big, frequent, and painful — are your opportunity set. Start there.

And one final note on fit. Not every opportunity is the right opportunity for every entrepreneur. The best business opportunity is not the largest market or the highest margin. It is the intersection of: a real problem in the market, your genuine ability to solve it, and your personal motivation to sustain the effort required.

An entrepreneur who has lived experience with the problem they are solving has advantages that research alone cannot provide. When you look at your opportunity list, notice which problems you feel not just interested in but compelled by. Compulsion, in the right context, is a business asset.

[PAUSE]

Questions for today. What is the most frequently recurring frustration you hear from people in your community? What trend in your city or region is creating new needs the market has not responded to? And what do you know how to do that most people around you have not had the opportunity to learn?

## Day 2: Better World Business — Profit and Purpose

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Day Two. Yesterday we talked about finding opportunities — problems, trends, and gaps in the market. Today, we are going to talk about what kind of business you build once you find that opportunity.

Specifically, we are going to talk about building a business that makes money and makes a difference at the same time.

[PAUSE]

For most of the history of modern capitalism, the definition of a successful business was straightforward: it made money. Profit was the primary metric. Everything else — the wellbeing of employees, the impact on the community, the sustainability of the practices used — was secondary at best.

This model produced enormous economic value. It also produced enormous human and environmental cost.

[PAUSE]

In 1994, a British consultant named John Elkington introduced the concept of the Triple Bottom Line. He proposed that businesses should measure success not by one bottom line — profit — but by three.

[GRAPHIC: The Triple Bottom Line:
PROFIT — Economic performance and financial sustainability
PEOPLE — Social performance: how the business affects workers, families, communities
PLANET — Environmental performance: resource use, waste, sustainability]

[ON CAMERA]

The Triple Bottom Line is not idealism dressed up as business strategy. It is an observation. Businesses that treat their employees well, strengthen their communities, and operate sustainably tend to be more resilient, earn deeper loyalty, and prove more durable over time than those that externalize these costs.

The triple bottom line is not a compromise of the profit motive. It is a more complete version of it.

[PAUSE]

Social entrepreneurship has become one of the most significant forces in global development, and Central Asia is not immune to its influence.

In Uzbekistan, textile cooperatives have emerged that provide fair wages to rural women while producing high-quality goods that command premium prices in export markets. The cooperatives generate profit for their members while addressing income inequality and preserving traditional craft knowledge. Neither the profit nor the social mission is incidental — each reinforces the other.

In Kazakhstan, mobile platforms connecting urban consumers directly with rural farmers have disrupted traditional agricultural distribution chains. Farmers receive better prices because they are no longer dependent on middlemen. Consumers get fresher produce at competitive prices. The platform earns a margin on every transaction. The social impact — improved rural incomes, reduced food waste — is not a charity project. It is the product of a well-designed market.

[B-ROLL: A textile cooperative — women working at looms. A farmer packaging produce for direct sale. A mobile app showing a marketplace interface.]

[ON CAMERA]

This is what "better world business" looks like in practice: not sacrifice, but alignment.

[PAUSE]

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals — seventeen interconnected targets ranging from poverty reduction to quality education to gender equality — have become a common language for measuring social impact. Central Asian governments have all formally committed to the SDGs, which means businesses explicitly aligned with SDG priorities may find it easier to access government support, development financing, and international partnership.

[GRAPHIC: Relevant SDGs:
- SDG 1: No Poverty
- SDG 4: Quality Education
- SDG 5: Gender Equality
- SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth
- SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities]

[ON CAMERA]

An entrepreneur whose business demonstrably improves access to education, promotes gender equality, or reduces inequalities has something concrete to say to funders and partners who are specifically looking for those outcomes.

[PAUSE]

The practical question for early-stage entrepreneurs is not "should my business change the world?" It is: "Is there a way to solve the problem I have identified that produces value beyond just financial returns?"

Sometimes the answer is no — and that is fine. A straightforward profitable business that pays fair wages and operates honestly is already contributing. But when deeper alignment is possible — when the structure of the business itself produces social benefit — it is worth designing for deliberately. Because it tends to produce better businesses, not just better intentions.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Trust is the currency of business in Central Asian societies. And businesses that are embedded in community wellbeing earn that trust more deeply and retain it more durably than those that are purely transactional.

A business that trains young people from the neighborhood. That sources locally. That gives back during community celebrations. That treats its lowest-paid workers with visible dignity. These behaviors generate the kind of word-of-mouth loyalty that no advertising budget can buy.

In the relationship-oriented business culture of this region, a business that is good for the community is a business the community wants to see succeed.

[PAUSE]

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "Social impact is not a layer you add on top of a business. It is most powerful when it is built into the core business model — so that making money and making a positive difference are the same activity, not separate ones."]

[ON CAMERA]

As you evaluate your opportunity this week, add these questions to your analysis: Who else benefits if this business succeeds, beyond the customers who pay for it? Are there workers, families, or communities who would experience improved conditions if this business scales? Is there a way to design the business so that its success is structurally connected to positive outcomes for the people around it?

These are not rhetorical questions. They are design questions. And the answers often reveal ways to make the business both more impactful and more durable.

[PAUSE]

Questions for today. What social problem exists in your community that your proposed business could meaningfully address as a natural consequence of solving the customer problem? Are there any SDGs your business concept is naturally aligned with? And what would need to be true for your business to be something your community is proud of, not just a service it uses?

Tomorrow, we cover the Four Hats framework — the tool you will use to evaluate your opportunity honestly and thoroughly.

## Day 3: The Four Hats — A Framework for Honest Feedback

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Welcome to Day Three. This is the last teaching day of Module One, and we are ending with what I believe is one of the most useful tools in the entire course: the Four Hats framework.

This is the tool that will help you evaluate your business idea honestly — without the two most common failure modes that kill good idea evaluation.

[PAUSE]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Here is the problem. Idea evaluation tends to happen in one of two modes, and both produce bad outcomes.

Mode one is uncritical enthusiasm. Everyone in the room loves the idea. Nobody raises the hard questions. The entrepreneur leaves the session energized but uninformed about real risks.

Mode two is undisciplined criticism. A free-for-all of objections, concerns, and doubts that mixes genuine risk identification with personal skepticism, competitive dynamics, and cultural norms about not appearing naive. The entrepreneur leaves the session discouraged and confused.

Neither mode produces the honest, structured analysis that good idea evaluation requires.

[PAUSE]

Edward de Bono, a Maltese psychologist and creativity researcher, solved this problem in the 1980s with his Six Thinking Hats framework. His insight was that the reason group thinking is unproductive is not that people are stupid. It is that they mix different types of thinking simultaneously. When one person is being enthusiastic and another is being critical in the same moment, they are not having a conversation. They are having a collision.

The solution? Separate the thinking modes in time. First, everyone thinks as optimists. Then everyone thinks as skeptics. Then everyone generates alternatives. The discipline of wearing the same hat at the same time is what makes it work.

[PAUSE]

Our Four Hats framework adapts de Bono's approach specifically for entrepreneurship.

[GRAPHIC: The Four Hats:
WHITE HAT — Facts and Data: "What do we actually know?"
BLACK HAT — Risks and Weaknesses: "What could go wrong?"
YELLOW HAT — Benefits and Upside: "What is genuinely exciting?"
GOLD HAT — Creative Alternatives: "What haven't we thought of?"]

[ON CAMERA]

Let me walk through each one.

The White Hat represents objective analysis — facts, data, and what is actually known. When wearing the White Hat, the question is: what do we actually know? Not what do we believe, or hope, or assume — what do we know from evidence, observation, or reliable data?

For a business idea, White Hat thinking covers: what market data exists? What are the actual demographics of the target customer? What are the known costs? What have similar businesses in similar markets actually achieved?

[PAUSE]

White Hat thinking is uncomfortable for many entrepreneurs because it reveals how much of what they call "knowledge" is actually assumption. That discomfort is productive. You cannot make good decisions based on what you wish were true.

[PAUSE]

The Black Hat represents critical, cautious thinking — risk identification and devil's advocacy. When wearing the Black Hat, the question is: what could go wrong? Where are the weaknesses? What are the three most dangerous risks?

Important: the Black Hat is not pessimism. It is not a judgment that the idea is bad. It is a specific analytical task — identify every significant risk factor so they can be addressed, mitigated, or accepted with eyes open.

The Black Hat is valuable precisely because the risks it identifies are the ones most likely to be invisible to an enthusiastic founder who is emotionally invested.

[PAUSE]

The Yellow Hat represents optimistic, value-focused thinking. When wearing the Yellow Hat, the question is: what is genuinely exciting about this? What are the real potential benefits? What value would this create if it works?

Yellow Hat thinking is not cheerleading. It is a serious analytical task focused on identifying the upside case with specificity. What would success look like for customers? For the community? For the entrepreneur?

This matters because without it, critical analysis can collapse into mere discouragement, and the entrepreneur loses sight of why the opportunity is worth pursuing despite its risks.

[PAUSE]

And the Gold Hat — this is an addition specific to our course — represents creative alternative thinking. When wearing the Gold Hat, the question is: what have we not thought of yet? Is there another way to approach this problem? What would a completely different business model look like for the same customer need?

Gold Hat thinking is generative rather than evaluative. It expands the solution space before you commit to a specific approach. Many of the best business innovations come from Gold Hat thinking — discovering that the customer need can be met through a mechanism completely different from the one you originally imagined.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Now, in Central Asian business culture, the Four Hats framework has a particular advantage. It depersonalizes criticism.

In high-context relational cultures, direct criticism of someone's idea can feel like a personal attack. The social cost of delivering honest feedback can be high enough that it gets suppressed entirely. The Four Hats reframes this completely: nobody is criticizing your idea. Everyone is collectively wearing the Black Hat, doing their job as careful risk analysts. The feedback is not personal — it is methodological.

This reframe often unlocks honesty that politeness would otherwise suppress. And that honesty is precisely what an entrepreneur needs before committing to a path.

[GRAPHIC: Callout box — "The Four Hats are most powerful when used in sequence, not simultaneously. Finish White Hat thinking completely before starting Black Hat. Never let different hats mix in the same discussion — that is when confusion and social conflict take over. Sequential thinking produces better results than mixed thinking every time."]

[ON CAMERA]

I want you to use this framework this week — not just in the worksheet, but if possible, with a group. Find two or three people you trust. Present your business idea. Then walk through the hats together, in order. White first, then Black, then Yellow, then Gold. You will be amazed at what surfaces when people are given permission to think in one mode at a time.

[PAUSE]

Questions for today. Which of the four hats is most natural for you to wear — and which is hardest? What does that tell you about your blind spots? What is one piece of honest Black Hat feedback you have been avoiding giving yourself about your business idea? And how could you use this framework with a trusted group to get better feedback than you would from a casual conversation?

## Day 4: Aijan's Story — Finding the Real Opportunity

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Story

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

This is the chapter of Aijan's story where everything changes. For two weeks, she has been struggling — selling daily bread and pastries to sparse foot traffic, losing money every day, working sixteen-hour shifts without asking whether any of it was pointed in the right direction.

And then she goes to tea at her neighbor's apartment.

[ANIMATION: An apartment building hallway. Aijan, looking tired, knocks on a door. Venera — a warm, older neighbor — opens it with a smile. Inside: a proper tea setup. Cloth on the table, jam in small dishes, bread, a steaming pot. Two women sitting down together.]

[ON CAMERA]

The realization did not come in the bakery. It came at her neighbor Venera's apartment, three weeks after Aijan had opened. Venera had invited her for tea — the proper kind, with jam and a cloth on the table — and Aijan had gone despite feeling exhausted and behind, because declining would have required an explanation she did not have the energy to give.

Venera poured and asked about the business. And Aijan answered honestly for the first time in weeks: it was harder than she had expected, the foot traffic was inconsistent, she was not sure the location was right, she was not sure the pricing was right, she was not sure what was right.

[ANIMATION: Close-up of Venera's face as she speaks. She looks thoughtful, then slightly animated as she remembers something. Aijan's expression shifts — she leans forward slightly, listening intently.]

[ON CAMERA]

Then Venera said something that changed things.

She said: "You know what I actually wanted last month? For my niece's engagement party. I wanted a beautiful custom cake — not huge, not expensive, something with dried roses and an inscription. I called everywhere in this part of the city. Nobody does it. The big places want you to order six weeks in advance and pay for something enormous. I ended up buying a box cake from the store and feeling embarrassed about it."

[PAUSE]

Aijan looked at her tea.

"How much would you have paid?" she asked.

Venera named a number. It was higher than the price of Aijan's most expensive item by a significant margin.

"And you said nobody offers this here?"

"Nobody reliable," Venera said. "There is one woman who does it from home but you never know if she will deliver. You cannot count on her for an important event."

[ANIMATION: Aijan driving home slowly. The city passing by outside the car window. Her expression is not excited — it is focused. Something is clarifying. A thought bubble or visual metaphor shows the shift: on one side, a generic bakery with shelves of bread; on the other side, a specific, beautiful custom cake for a celebration.]

[ON CAMERA]

Aijan drove home slowly. Something was clarifying. The business she had opened was the business she had imagined: a retail bakery serving everyone, selling daily bread and pastries and hoping foot traffic would build. But that was not the opportunity.

The opportunity was something specific: custom celebration cakes for the occasions that mattered, in the part of the city that had no reliable option, for customers who needed to trust the person they were ordering from.

[PAUSE]

It was not an enormous market. But it was a real one. A specific one. A market she could actually serve with what she knew how to make, for customers who actually had the problem she could solve.

[ANIMATION: Aijan at her kitchen table with a notebook. She is writing, sketching, planning. Her expression has changed — from exhaustion to clarity. She draws a rough business model: custom cakes → celebrations → word of mouth → personal trust → higher price point.]

[ON CAMERA]

She was not discovering a new business idea. She was discovering the one that had been in front of her all along, waiting for her to ask the right questions.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

And notice — this did not happen because of a brilliant insight or a lucky break. It happened because she went to tea. She sat with a real person. She listened. And that person, unprompted, described a real problem that nobody was solving.

That is opportunity identification. Not invention. Noticing.

Aijan's story continues next week. The question now is: can she turn this observation into a real business? She has the opportunity. She still needs the model.

## KYRGYZ VERSION - Story: Aijan Finds the Real Opportunity

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Story (Central Asia version)

---

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

This is the chapter where Aijan's story changes direction. She has been delivering five different lunch items to scattered offices, working from her small apartment kitchen, and the numbers are not adding up. But this week, she has a conversation that shows her what she has been missing.

[ANIMATION: A simple office building canteen. Aluminum table, fluorescent lighting. Aijan has arrived early to make a delivery. She is sitting across from a man — Nurlan, a logistics coordinator — who is eating instant noodles from a paper cup, scrolling his phone. She sits down and begins asking questions, carefully, neutrally.]

[ON CAMERA]

The realization did not come while she was cooking. It came at the aluminum table in the canteen of the office building on Chui Avenue where she had been delivering lunches to four office workers for two weeks. She had arrived early to make a delivery and ended up sitting across from a logistics coordinator named Nurlan, who was eating instant noodles out of a paper cup and scrolling his phone.

She asked him, with the careful neutrality her program facilitator had taught her, what he usually did for lunch. He gestured at the noodles.

She asked what he wished were different.

He looked at her for a moment as though the question were strange, then set down his phone.

[ANIMATION: Close-up of Nurlan speaking. He becomes animated as he describes the problem. Behind him, a visual of the ashkana downstairs — plov sitting in pans under heat lamps, dry lagman, workers eating quickly and without enthusiasm.]

[ON CAMERA]

"Something that does not make me feel bad by two o'clock," he said. "Something that tastes like actual food. The ashkana downstairs reheats everything — the plov is from yesterday, the lagman is dry. But I do not have time to go anywhere else. By the time I get my coat and go somewhere decent, my lunch break is over."

She asked if she could ask a few more questions. He said yes. Over the next fifteen minutes she learned things she had not thought to ask: that he would pay a hundred eighty to two hundred som for a fresh meal, that he specifically wanted something that would not sit heavy in the afternoon, that he trusted food more when he could see it was freshly made rather than reheated, and that he would be willing to order through WhatsApp or Telegram the morning of if he knew it would arrive by twelve-thirty.

[ANIMATION: Aijan walking to other offices in the building — knocking on doors, sitting down with workers, asking questions, taking notes. Numbers appearing: 14 out of 15 workers describe the same problem. Same frustrations. Same acceptance of the situation as unchangeable.]

[ON CAMERA]

She walked to three other offices in the building afterward and asked the same questions. The numbers were consistent. Fourteen out of fifteen workers she spoke to that week described some version of the same problem: the ashkana food was acceptable but not good, the better options required travel that did not fit in a lunch break, and they had simply accepted the situation as unchangeable.

Nobody had offered them an alternative.

[ANIMATION: Aijan at home that evening. A blank notebook open. She writes slowly, crossing out general descriptions and replacing them with specific ones. The notebook shows: "Fresh home-cooked meals — real ingredients — cooked same morning — delivered to offices within specific radius — by 12:30 — ordered by WhatsApp/Telegram by 10 AM." It is specific. It is clear.]

[ON CAMERA]

That evening Aijan sat with a blank notebook and tried to describe what she was actually building. Not a general food delivery service. Not a five-item menu for anyone who wanted lunch. Something specific: fresh home-cooked meals — the kind you actually feel full from, using real ingredients, cooked the same morning — delivered to offices within a specific radius by twelve-thirty, ordered by WhatsApp or Telegram by ten in the morning.

She thought about her mother's recipes. Her mother, who taught school near Tokmok, made food that people drove from nearby villages to eat. The flavor of her lagman came from the fact that she made the noodles herself and her broth had been on the stove since seven in the morning. That quality — the thing that distinguished home cooking from ashkana reheating — was exactly the gap Nurlan and his colleagues were describing.

[PAUSE]

The business she had been trying to build was general. The opportunity in front of her was specific: serve the workers in these office buildings, within this neighborhood, with her mother's recipes, by twelve-thirty, every weekday. She could start with two buildings. She could start with twelve portions. She could start next Monday.

She was not inventing something new. She was noticing what had been in front of her all along, waiting for her to stop guessing and start listening.

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Aijan's story continues next week. Notice how this turn happened — not through planning, but through asking. Through sitting down with a real customer and listening without defending her original idea. That is what "get out of the building" looks like when it works.

## Day 5: Action Steps — The Opportunity Canvas and Four Hats Checkpoint #1

**Video Length:** ~12 minutes | **Type:** Action

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to Day Five. This is a big action day — the biggest so far in the course. You have two things to accomplish this week. First, the Opportunity Canvas worksheet. Second, your first Four Hats Checkpoint.

Let me walk you through both.

[SCREEN SHARE: Opportunity Canvas worksheet — Section 1: Opportunity Identification]

[ON CAMERA]

Section one asks you to identify three potential business opportunities. Not one — three. I want you to have options before you commit.

For each opportunity, describe the problem or gap. What is the unmet need? Who experiences it? How often? Then describe the business that could address it — a possible business model in two or three sentences.

Where do you find these three opportunities? Use everything we covered on Day One. Look at your problem journal if you kept one. Think about the frustrations you have personally observed. Consider the trends shaping your community. Do a quick PEST analysis. Remember: problems that are big, frequent, and painful are where real opportunities live.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 2 — PEST Analysis]

[ON CAMERA]

Section two is a PEST analysis for your most promising opportunity. Go through each of the four forces.

Political: what regulations, policies, or government programs affect this opportunity? Are there licensing requirements? Import restrictions? Government development programs in your sector?

Economic: what are the income trends in your target market? What are your costs? Is demand growing or shrinking?

Social: what cultural trends or demographic shifts affect this? Is the middle class growing? Are lifestyle habits changing? Is women's economic participation increasing?

Technological: what new tools enable this opportunity? Smartphone adoption? Social commerce platforms? New production methods?

Be specific to your community. A PEST analysis of your neighborhood is more useful than one of your entire country.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 3 — Social Impact Assessment]

[ON CAMERA]

Section three evaluates the social impact of your chosen opportunity. Who else benefits if this business succeeds? How does it strengthen your community? And — optionally but usefully — which Sustainable Development Goals does it align with? This is especially relevant if you plan to pursue development financing or international partnership down the road.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 4 — Four Hats Evaluation]

[ON CAMERA]

Section four is where you put on the Four Hats. One at a time. In order.

White Hat first. What do you know for certain about this opportunity? List only facts you can verify — customer demographics, known costs, observable competitor behavior, market data you have actually seen. Not what you believe. What you know.

Black Hat second. What are the three biggest risks or failure modes? Be specific. What could go wrong? Where are your assumptions weakest? What has killed similar businesses before?

Yellow Hat third. What is genuinely exciting about this opportunity? What value would it create for customers? What is the best realistic scenario if this works?

Gold Hat fourth. What is one approach to this problem that you have not yet considered? Could the same problem be solved with a completely different business model? What would that look like?

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 5 — Opportunity Selection]

[ON CAMERA]

And section five — you make your choice. Which opportunity are you going to pursue, and why? Give two or three specific reasons based on your analysis. And then: what is the first test you will run to validate that this opportunity is real?

[PAUSE]

Now, let me talk about the Four Hats Checkpoint.

[GRAPHIC: Four Hats Checkpoint #1 — End of Module 1]

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

This is your first of four checkpoints across the entire course. These happen at the end of Weeks Three, Six, Nine, and Twelve. They are stress tests for your business idea, and they matter.

Here is how it works. You are going to present your business opportunity and explain why you believe it is a real problem worth solving. Include who the customer is, what problem they experience, and why existing solutions are inadequate.

Then you — or ideally a group of peers or a mentor — evaluate the idea through each hat.

[GRAPHIC: Checkpoint #1 prompts:
WHITE HAT: What facts and data actually support this opportunity? What do we KNOW — not believe, not hope — about the customer, the problem, the market?
BLACK HAT: What are the 3 biggest risks? Where are assumptions weakest? What has caused similar businesses to fail?
YELLOW HAT: What are the 3 most compelling potential benefits? What value would this create if it works?
