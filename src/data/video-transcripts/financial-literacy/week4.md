The debt inventory is brutally simple. Here's what you do. You sit down with your bank statements, your credit card bills, your loan documents, and you make a list. Every debt. No exceptions. Credit cards. Store cards. Buy-now-pay-later accounts. Car loans. Student loans. Medical bills. Personal loans from family or friends. Everything.

For each one, you write down five things: the creditor name, the type of debt, the current balance, the interest rate, and the minimum monthly payment.

But here's the paradox: the moment you see the full number is also the moment you gain power over it. Before the inventory, the debt was infinite and unknowable. After the inventory, it's finite. [ON CAMERA]

There's another reason this inventory matters, and it goes deeper than dollars and cents. Every dollar you owe is a dollar you don't control. It's already spoken for. Already claimed by someone else. The inventory is the first step toward reclaiming that freedom.

And here's something else that happens when you do this exercise. Some people discover debts they'd forgotten about. A medical bill that went to collections. A store card with a small balance that's been quietly accruing interest for years. A personal loan from a friend they'd mentally written off. The inventory forces honesty. And honesty, as uncomfortable as it is, is the only path to freedom.

Because here's what I believe: the weight you feel about your debt isn't just financial. It's emotional. It's the shame, the anxiety, the sense that you're always behind, always trying to catch up, never quite free. Those chains are just as real as the debt itself.

Facing the full truth about your debt is an act of courage. It's choosing to see rather than hide. It's the first step toward breaking chains that may have held you -- or your family -- for years. And breaking those chains isn't just about numbers. It's about reclaiming the person you were meant to be.

[PAUSE]

So this week, your assignment is clear. Sit down. Pull out the statements. And write it all down. Every debt. No exceptions. We'll talk about what to do with that list in our next lesson.

## Day 2: Snowball vs. Avalanche

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

All right, so you've done the hard part. You've written it all down. You know the number. You've faced the truth. And now comes the question that changes everything: how do you pay it off?

There are two main strategies that personal finance experts recommend, and they have competing philosophies. The first is called the Debt Snowball. The second is called the Debt Avalanche. Both work. But they work for different reasons. And by the end of this lesson, you're going to pick the one that's right for you.

[GRAPHIC: Split screen -- "DEBT SNOWBALL" on left, "DEBT AVALANCHE" on right]

[ON CAMERA]

Let's start with the Debt Snowball. The snowball method says this: ignore interest rates. I know that sounds counterintuitive, but stick with me. You take all your debts and you order them from smallest balance to largest balance. You pay the minimum on everything except the smallest debt. And then you throw every extra dollar you can find at that smallest debt. When it's gone -- when it's completely paid off -- you take the payment you were making on it and roll it into the next smallest debt. The payment gets bigger each time, like a snowball rolling downhill, gathering mass and momentum.

[B-ROLL: Animation of a snowball rolling downhill, getting larger with each rotation]

[ON CAMERA]

Now, why does the Snowball work? Because personal finance is 80 percent behavior and 20 percent math. Let me say that again. Eighty percent behavior. Twenty percent math. The Snowball gives you quick wins. When you pay off that first debt -- even if it's just 800 dollars or 1,200 dollars -- something changes inside you. You feel it. You taste freedom. That first victory proves the plan works, and it fuels your determination to keep going. The momentum becomes psychological, not just financial.

[PAUSE]

Now let's talk about the Debt Avalanche. The Avalanche takes a different approach. It says: order your debts from highest interest rate to lowest interest rate. Pay minimums on everything except the highest-rate debt. Attack that one first. This method saves you the most money in interest over time because you're eliminating the most expensive debt first. Mathematically, it's the optimal strategy.

[GRAPHIC: Example comparison -- Snowball order vs. Avalanche order with same debts, showing different payoff sequences]

[ON CAMERA]

So which one should you choose? Here's the honest answer: the best debt payoff plan is the one you'll actually finish.

[PAUSE]

If you're the kind of person who needs quick wins to stay motivated -- and most of us are, by the way -- the Snowball is for you. If you're analytical and motivated by efficiency, if you love spreadsheets and the idea of saving every last penny in interest excites you, the Avalanche might work better.

But here's what I've seen: most people -- especially those who've tried to pay off debt before and quit -- need the Snowball. They need to see progress fast.

[SERIOUS TONE]

There's a deeper reason the Snowball works for most people. Debt elimination isn't a sprint. It's a marathon. And marathons are won by people who keep going when it gets hard. The Snowball builds the habit of winning. Each paid-off debt is a milestone, a celebration, a reminder that you're making real progress. By the time you're three or four debts in, the momentum is unstoppable.

The Avalanche, by contrast, can feel slower at first. If your highest-interest debt is also your largest balance, it might take months or even years to see that first victory. For people who are already discouraged, already exhausted from years of minimum payments, that delay can be fatal to motivation. The math is better, but the psychology is harder.

[GRAPHIC: "The Compromise Strategy: Start Snowball → Build Momentum → Switch to Avalanche"]

[ON CAMERA]

Here's a compromise some people use, and I think it's pretty smart. Start with the Snowball to build momentum, then switch to the Avalanche once you've knocked out a few debts and proven to yourself that you can do this. The early wins give you confidence. The later efficiency saves you money. Best of both worlds.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Think about this: are you more motivated by quick wins, or by mathematical efficiency? Have you tried to pay off debt before? What made you quit? Which method feels right for your personality and your situation?

There's something powerful about persistence in the face of a mountain that looks too high to climb. Debt can feel that way -- overwhelming, insurmountable, like no matter how hard you work, you'll never reach the top. That feeling is one of the reasons so many people give up.

But here's what the Debt Snowball teaches. Progress is made one step at a time. You don't have to see the top of the mountain to take the next step. You just have to keep moving. The first debt you pay off might be small, but it proves something crucial: the mountain can be climbed. And once you know that, hope replaces despair.

[PAUSE]

Hope is a powerful force. It's the difference between giving up and pressing on. And sometimes, the most important thing you can do is choose a strategy that keeps hope alive long enough for you to finish the journey.

So pick your method. Snowball, Avalanche, or the hybrid approach. Write it down. And get ready, because tomorrow we're going to talk about how to supercharge whichever method you choose -- by finding extra money to throw at that debt and accelerating your payoff.

## Day 3: Accelerating Payoff

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Okay. You know what you owe. You've picked your method -- Snowball, Avalanche, or the hybrid. Now comes the question that will determine how fast you get free: how much extra money can you throw at your debt?

Here's the reality. If you're only making minimum payments, you'll be paying for decades. That's not an exaggeration. A credit card with a 5,000-dollar balance at 20 percent interest, if you only make the minimum payment, will take you over 25 years to pay off, and you'll pay more than 10,000 dollars in interest alone. You'll pay double the original amount. Minimum payments are designed to keep you in debt forever.

But if you can add an extra 100, 200, or 500 dollars a month, you'll cut years off your timeline. Every extra dollar matters. Every single one.

[PAUSE]

So where do you find that extra money? The answer is uncomfortable but true: you make sacrifices.

[SERIOUS TONE]

This season of your life isn't about comfort. It's about intensity. You sell things you don't need. You cut expenses you've been justifying. You pick up side income. You negotiate lower interest rates. You redirect every windfall -- tax refunds, work bonuses, birthday money, gifts -- straight to debt. All of it.

[B-ROLL: Someone walking through their house, picking up items -- old electronics, books, sports equipment -- and putting them in a box]

[ON CAMERA]

Let's start with what you already own. Walk through your house this week and ask yourself: what could I sell? Old electronics. Furniture you're not using. Clothes that don't fit. Tools in the garage. Kids' toys they've outgrown. Books, DVDs, sports equipment. People have raised thousands of dollars by selling things that were gathering dust in their closet. Every dollar from a sale is a dollar that shortens your debt timeline.

[GRAPHIC: List -- "Where to Find Extra Money: 1. Sell what you own, 2. Cut expenses radically, 3. Pick up side income, 4. Negotiate interest rates, 5. Redirect every windfall"]

[ON CAMERA]

Next, look at your budget. Where can you cut? And I don't mean small tweaks. I mean radical, temporary sacrifice. Cancel subscriptions you don't absolutely need. Stop eating out. Cut the cable. Shop at discount grocery stores. Drive less. The goal is to squeeze every possible dollar out of your current income and redirect it to debt. This intensity won't last forever. But it has to last long enough to kill the debt.

[PAUSE]

Then there's side income. We live in the gig economy. There are dozens of ways to earn extra money. Rideshare driving. Food delivery. Freelancing. Tutoring. Dog walking. Yard work. Online selling. Even an extra 500 dollars a month -- that's just 125 dollars a week -- can cut years off a debt payoff plan. The work is hard, the hours are long. But the freedom on the other side is worth it.

[B-ROLL: Montage of gig work -- driving, delivering food, mowing a lawn, selling items online]

[ON CAMERA]

Now here's one that most people overlook: negotiating with your creditors. If you have high-interest credit card debt, call the company and ask for a lower rate. This is a real thing you can do. If you've been a decent customer with a history of on-time payments, they'll often reduce your rate by several percentage points. That lower rate means more of your payment goes to principal instead of interest. It's a five-minute phone call that can save you hundreds or thousands of dollars.

Another option is balance transfers. Some credit cards offer zero percent interest for 12 to 18 months on transferred balances. If you can transfer a high-interest balance to a zero-percent card and pay it off during the promotional period, you save a massive amount in interest. But be careful -- if you don't pay it off in time, the interest can come roaring back. This strategy requires discipline.

[SERIOUS TONE]

[PAUSE]

Here's the key mindset shift for this entire season of your life. Every financial decision you make right now should be viewed through one lens: debt elimination.

When you get a raise, don't inflate your lifestyle -- throw it at debt. When you get a tax refund, don't take a vacation -- throw it at debt. When someone offers to pay you for a weekend project, say yes -- and throw it at debt. This is war. And wars are won by people who stay focused on the mission until it's over.

[PAUSE]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

So let me leave you with some questions. What could you sell this month to put toward debt? Where can you cut expenses radically, even if just temporarily? What side income could you pick up for the next six to twelve months? And here's the one I really want you to sit with: how would your life change if you were completely debt-free a year from now?

[PAUSE]

Close your eyes for a second and picture it. No credit card payments. No student loan payment. No owing your dad or your brother-in-law. Every dollar you earn is yours to decide what to do with. That's what you're fighting for. That's what the sacrifices are about. Not deprivation for its own sake -- freedom. Real, tangible, life-changing freedom.

You are not your debt. You are someone worth setting free. And the fight starts now.

Column two: Type of Debt. What kind of debt is it? Credit card, auto loan, student loan, medical bill, personal loan, store card, buy-now-pay-later. Be specific.

Column three: Current Balance. The amount you owe today. Not what you originally borrowed. What you owe right now. Log in to the account and get the exact number.

Column four: Interest Rate. This is the annual percentage rate -- the APR. It's on your statement. If you have a personal loan from a family member with no formal interest, write zero, but still list it.

Column five: Minimum Payment. The smallest amount you're required to pay each month to stay current.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN SHARE: Example filled-in inventory with 5-6 debts listed, totals at bottom]

[ON CAMERA]

Fill in every row. Don't skip any debt, no matter how small or how embarrassing. When you're done, add up the balance column. That's your total debt number. Write it at the bottom and circle it.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Take a breath. Whatever that number is, it's finite. It has a boundary. And we're going to destroy it.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 2 -- "Debt Snowball Order"]

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Section two is where the fight begins. The Debt Snowball Order. Take all those debts from section one and rewrite them -- this time in order from smallest balance to largest balance. Ignore interest rates. Just look at the balance.

For each debt, you'll write the snowball order number -- one, two, three, and so on. The debt name. The balance. And then the monthly attack payment, which is the minimum payment plus any extra you can throw at it.

Your number one debt -- the smallest balance -- gets the biggest attack payment. Everything else gets the minimum. When number one is dead, you roll that entire payment into number two. That's the snowball.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 3 -- "Accelerating Payoff"]

[ON CAMERA]

Section three is where you find the fuel. Four questions. How much extra can you pull from budget cuts? How much can you raise by selling items? How much side income can you generate per month? And are there any expected windfalls coming, like a tax refund or a bonus?

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 4 -- "Your Debt-Free Date"]

[ON CAMERA]

And section four -- this is the payoff. Literally. Take your smallest debt balance. Write it down. Take your monthly payment -- minimum plus extra. Write that down. Divide the balance by the payment. Round up. That's how many months until your first debt is dead.

Then write the date. Not "someday." A real date. If it's four months away, count the months on a calendar and write it down. June 2026. August 2026. Whatever it is. That date is your target. Put it on your fridge. Put it on your phone. Put it where you'll see it every single day.

Your other action items for this week: make one extra payment toward your smallest debt, even if it's just five dollars. And take on zero new debt this week -- no credit card charges, no buy-now-pay-later. Nothing. The wall stops here.
