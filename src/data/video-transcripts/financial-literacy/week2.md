# Week 2: Identity & Work

## Day 1: The Identity Trap

**Video Length:** ~14 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Welcome to week two. Last week, we took a hard look at our financial reality. We filled out the snapshot. We identified our money scripts. We committed to the ownership mindset. That was foundational work, and if you did it, you should feel good about that.

This week, we're going deeper. We're going to talk about something that affects every financial decision you make, whether you realize it or not. It's not a budgeting technique. It's not a savings trick. It's a belief. And for most of us, it's a lie.

The lie is this: I am what I earn.

[PAUSE]

You may have never said that out loud. But it shows up in subtle ways. When someone asks what you do, you answer with your job title -- as if your profession defines you. When you see someone driving a nicer car or living in a bigger house, you feel a pit in your stomach, like you're falling behind in a race you didn't know you were running. When you lose a job or take a pay cut, it doesn't just feel like a financial setback -- it feels like you've lost part of yourself.

This is what I call the identity trap. And it is one of the most destructive lies in modern culture.

[B-ROLL: Someone scrolling Instagram, seeing vacation photos, a new car post, a home renovation reveal. Their expression shifts from neutral to deflated.]

[ON CAMERA]

The identity trap turns every financial decision into a referendum on your value as a person. It makes you chase promotions you don't want, buy things you don't need, and compete with people who aren't even thinking about you. It replaces contentment with chronic anxiety because there's always someone earning more, owning more, achieving more.

And the comparison cycle that fuels this trap is vicious. You see a highlight reel on social media -- a friend's vacation, a colleague's promotion, a neighbor's new car. You compare their best to your average. You feel inadequate. So you spend money to close the gap -- a new outfit, a fancy dinner, something to prove you're keeping up. The spending brings temporary relief followed by guilt and regret. And then the cycle repeats.

[GRAPHIC: Circular diagram showing the comparison cycle:
See highlight reel --> Compare their best to your average --> Feel inadequate --> Spend to close the gap --> Temporary relief --> Guilt/regret --> See highlight reel again]

[ON CAMERA]

Social media is gasoline on this fire. Every scroll is an invitation to compare. Every post is curated to look effortless and enviable. Nobody posts their credit card statement next to their vacation photos. Nobody shows the debt behind the new car. You're comparing your behind-the-scenes to everyone else's highlight reel. And you will lose that game every single time.

[PAUSE]

But here's what makes the identity trap truly insidious: it's not just about wanting more money. It's about tying your sense of self-worth to something fundamentally unstable. Your income can change overnight -- a layoff, a recession, a health crisis. If your identity is built on that foundation, what happens when the foundation cracks?

People who've lost their jobs often describe it like losing themselves. That's the identity trap at work. Your job was never supposed to carry that much weight.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

The way out of the identity trap is to untangle your worth from your wallet. This doesn't mean you stop working hard or pursuing excellence. It means you stop letting your bank account tell you who you are.

One of the most freeing realizations you can have is this: your value was established long before you earned your first dollar, and it will remain long after you've spent your last one. You are not what you earn. You are not your job title. You are not your net worth.

Gratitude is the antidote to comparison. When you catch yourself measuring your life against someone else's, stop and name three things you're grateful for. Not abstract things -- specific, tangible gifts in your life right now. The comparison trap loses its power when you shift from scarcity thinking to abundance thinking. Scarcity says there's never enough and you're always behind. Abundance says you have what you need for today, and that's enough.

[PAUSE]

Some people believe that their worth isn't something they earn or lose -- it's something that was given to them. If you were created with inherent value, then your job isn't to prove your worth. It's to live out of the worth you already have. That shift changes everything. You work not to prove yourself but to steward what you've been given. You earn not to validate yourself but to provide and to bless others.

Let me ask you something to sit with before tomorrow: If your income doubled tomorrow, what would actually change about how you see yourself? If the answer is "a lot," you might be deeper in the identity trap than you think.

I'll see you tomorrow.

## Day 2: Maximizing Your Work

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome back. Yesterday we talked about the identity trap -- the lie that your worth equals your net worth. Today we're going to flip the script. Instead of talking about what your work means for your identity, we're going to talk about what you can do with your work. Because whether you realize it or not, your current job is one of the most powerful assets you have. The question is: are you managing that asset intentionally, or are you letting it manage you?

[PAUSE]

Most people treat their job like something that happens to them. They clock in, do what's required, clock out, and repeat. They see work as a transaction: time for money. And while that's technically true, it's a tragic underestimation of what work can be.

Your current job -- whatever it is -- is not just a paycheck. It's an asset you can develop, grow, and leverage. Here's a question that should make you think: if you treated your career like an investment portfolio, what would you change this year?

[B-ROLL: Someone at a desk looking bored, then a cut to someone at a desk who's engaged, taking notes, actively participating in a meeting]

[ON CAMERA]

Maximizing your work starts with a mindset shift. Instead of asking "what does my employer owe me?" ask "what value can I create?" Instead of waiting for someone to notice your effort, take ownership of your growth. Instead of seeing your job as a box you're stuck in, see it as a platform you're building on.

This doesn't mean becoming a workaholic. It means being strategic. It means treating your career like an investment that compounds over time.

[PAUSE]

Let me introduce you to a concept called skill-stacking. This is one of the most powerful ideas in career development, and almost nobody talks about it. You don't need to be the best in the world at one thing. You need to be very good at a combination of things that makes you uniquely valuable.

[GRAPHIC: Venn diagram showing overlapping skills:
"Graphic Design" + "Copywriting" = "More valuable than either alone"
"Sales" + "Data Analytics" = "Competitive edge"
"Teaching" + "Technology" = "Stands out"]

[ON CAMERA]

A graphic designer who can also write copy is more valuable than one who can only design. A salesperson who understands data analytics has an edge over one who doesn't. A teacher who knows how to use technology effectively stands out.

Look at your current role and ask: what adjacent skills could I develop that would make me indispensable? Not a whole new career. Just one additional skill that complements what you already do.

[ON CAMERA]

Now let's talk about something most people are terrified of: negotiation. Specifically, asking for a raise.

Here's the truth: your employer expects you to negotiate. It's part of the game. If you never ask for a raise, you're leaving money on the table -- money that compounds over the life of your career. Think about it. If you earn fifty thousand dollars and you negotiate a five thousand dollar raise, that's not just five thousand dollars this year. Over twenty years, with normal cost-of-living increases and compounding, that one conversation could be worth a hundred thousand dollars or more.

The key to effective negotiation isn't aggression or entitlement. It's value demonstration. Before you ask for more, make sure you can answer this question: how have I made this organization more valuable since I started? Bring data. Bring examples. Make it easy for them to say yes.

[PAUSE]

Sometimes maximizing your work means knowing when to leave. Not every job is worth staying in. If you've hit a ceiling, if the culture is toxic, if you're being underpaid and undervalued despite your best efforts -- it might be time to move on. But here's the critical distinction: you should leave toward something better, not away from something bad. Don't quit in frustration. Quit with a plan. Update your resume. Build your network. Line up the next opportunity. Strategic career moves compound. Emotional career moves often backfire.

[B-ROLL: Someone updating a LinkedIn profile, shaking hands at a networking event, polishing a resume]

[ON CAMERA]

Your work has dignity. Whether you're stocking shelves, managing a team, building websites, or teaching kids, your work matters. It contributes to the functioning of the world. It provides for your family. And when done with intention, it becomes more than a transaction -- it becomes a contribution.

There's an old idea worth considering: that all work -- even mundane, difficult, or thankless work -- can be done as an act of purpose. Ancient wisdom teaches that whatever you do, you should do it wholeheartedly, as if working for something greater than any human employer. If you truly believed that your work was seen and valued by something greater than your boss, how would that change your attitude on a Monday morning?

[PAUSE]

Think about this: what's one skill adjacent to your current role that would make you more valuable? And when was the last time you asked for a raise or promotion? If it's been more than a year, ask yourself why.

Tomorrow, we're talking about income diversification -- how to build multiple streams of income without burning out. I'll see you then.

## Day 3: Income Diversification

**Video Length:** ~13 minutes | **Type:** Lesson

---

[ON CAMERA]

Welcome back. We've talked about the identity trap and how to maximize your current work. Today we're going to talk about something that might feel ambitious, but it's actually incredibly practical: income diversification.

The industrial age taught us to find one good job and stay there for forty years. That model is dead. The modern economy rewards people who can create value in multiple ways. But before you start panicking about needing five side hustles and zero sleep, let me be clear -- income diversification is about thinking strategically, not running yourself into the ground.

A single income stream is a single point of failure. Multiple streams create stability and opportunity. That's the goal.

[PAUSE]

I want to teach you a framework I call Maintain, Grow, Create. It's the simplest and most effective way to think about income diversification.

[GRAPHIC: Three-tier pyramid:
Bottom tier (largest): "MAINTAIN - Protect your current income"
Middle tier: "GROW - Increase what you're already earning"
Top tier: "CREATE - Explore new income streams"]

[ON CAMERA]

Maintain means protecting and stewarding your current income. Don't take it for granted. Show up. Do excellent work. Be reliable. This is your foundation. If you can't maintain what you have, you're not ready to grow it.

Grow means looking for ways to increase your current income. This could be asking for a raise, pursuing a promotion, getting certified in a new skill, or taking on additional responsibilities that come with additional pay. The key is to grow from where you are, not to jump ship prematurely.

Create means exploring new income streams beyond your primary job. This is where side hustles, freelancing, consulting, and entrepreneurship come into play.

[ON CAMERA]

Here's what's beautiful about the Create category: it leverages skills you already have. You're not starting from zero. If you're a graphic designer by day, you can take freelance projects in the evening. If you're good with numbers, you can offer bookkeeping services. If you're a natural teacher, you can tutor or create online courses. The goal isn't to replace your primary income immediately. The goal is to test, learn, and build something that could grow over time.

[PAUSE]

But here's the biggest mistake people make with income diversification: trying to do too much too fast. They burn out. They neglect their primary job. They sacrifice time with family. They chase every shiny opportunity and finish nothing.

[SERIOUS TONE]

Sustainable income diversification requires guardrails. Before you say yes to a new income opportunity, ask yourself four questions.

[GRAPHIC: Four guardrail questions:
1. Does this align with my skills and interests?
2. Does this fit within my available time without sacrificing rest or relationships?
3. Is this rooted in stewardship or driven by greed?
4. Can I commit to this for at least 90 days?]

[ON CAMERA]

If you can't answer yes to all four, it's probably not the right opportunity right now. Not every opportunity is the right opportunity. Some look great on paper but lead to burnout, broken relationships, or ethical compromise. Discernment matters.

The goal isn't just to make more money. The goal is to steward what you have well, create value for others, and build a financial foundation that supports the life you want to live -- not the life you're trying to prove to others.

[PAUSE]

Income diversification also creates something invaluable: options. When you have multiple streams, losing one doesn't devastate you. You have breathing room. You have flexibility. You can take risks you couldn't take before. You can say no to opportunities that don't align with your values. Financial margin creates freedom -- not just freedom to spend, but freedom to choose.

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Here's the deeper question worth sitting with: what if your skills and talents were given to you for a reason? What if they're not just for your benefit but for the benefit of others? When you create additional income by solving problems, meeting needs, and serving people well, you're not just making money. You're fulfilling a purpose. That's the difference between income diversification rooted in stewardship and income diversification rooted in greed. One is about contribution. The other is about accumulation.

There's a balance to strike, though. Workaholism masquerading as stewardship is still workaholism. Rest is not lazy. The goal is not to work more -- it's to work smarter and with purpose. Multiple income streams should create margin in your life, not eliminate it.

[PAUSE]

Before story day tomorrow, I want you to think about this: what's one skill or talent you have that others would pay for? Don't overthink it. What do people already ask you for help with? That's often your starting point.

See you tomorrow.

## Day 5: Action -- 90-Day Income Map Worksheet

**Video Length:** ~10 minutes | **Type:** Action / Worksheet

---

[ON CAMERA]

[ENTHUSIASTIC]

Welcome to action day. Today we're going to build your 90-Day Income Map. This is one of my favorite exercises in the whole course because it takes you from reactive to proactive. Instead of just wondering about your financial future, you're going to map it out.

Pull up the worksheet. Let's go through it section by section.

[SCREEN SHARE: 90-Day Income Map worksheet overview showing five sections: Maintain, Grow, Create, 90-Day Goal, Guardrail Checklist]

[ON CAMERA]

This worksheet has five sections, and we're going to use that Maintain, Grow, Create framework we talked about yesterday. Let's start at the foundation.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 1 - MAINTAIN: Current Income]

[ON CAMERA]

Section one: Maintain. List your current income sources. Your primary job. Any secondary income. For each one, write down the monthly amount. Then -- and this is the important part -- write down one specific action you'll take to protect and steward that income.

For your primary job, that might be "complete the training my boss recommended" or "ask for feedback on my performance." For a secondary income, it might be "maintain my three regular clients" or "keep my freelance portfolio updated."

Don't skip this section because it seems obvious. Most people lose income they already have because they take it for granted. The best foundation for growth is protecting what you already have.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 2 - GROW: Growth Opportunities]

[ON CAMERA]

Section two: Grow. What specific steps could you take to increase your current income in the next ninety days? Write down one concrete growth opportunity. Maybe it's asking for a raise. Maybe it's pursuing a certification. Maybe it's volunteering for a higher-visibility project at work.

Then give yourself a deadline. "By when?" Not "someday." A specific date.

What stands in the way? Name the obstacle. Is it fear? Timing? Not knowing who to ask? Be honest.

And who can help you? A mentor, a manager, a friend who's done it before. You don't have to figure this out alone.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 3 - CREATE: New Income Exploration]

[ON CAMERA]

Section three: Create. This is where it gets exciting. What skills or talents do you have that could become new income streams?

Write down one or two skills. Think about what people already ask you for help with. That's usually a clue. Then write down a potential opportunity for each skill and the very first step you'd need to take to explore it.

If you're a good writer, the opportunity might be freelance blog writing, and the first step might be creating a writing sample. If you're handy with tools, the opportunity might be small repair services, and the first step might be posting on a local marketplace.

Don't overthink this. Don't try to build a business plan. Just identify the possibility and the first step.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 4 - 90-Day Goal]

[ON CAMERA]

Section four: Your 90-Day Goal. Write down your current monthly income. Then write down your goal monthly income ninety days from now. The difference is your Monthly Increase Goal.

Be realistic but be bold. If you're making four thousand a month and you want to make forty thousand, that's not a ninety-day goal -- that's a fantasy. But if you're making four thousand and you want to make forty-five hundred, that's a real, achievable stretch. That extra five hundred a month is six thousand dollars a year. That's not small.

Then write down one thing you will do this week. Not this month. This week. Be specific and actionable.

[SCREEN SHARE: Section 5 - Guardrail Checklist]

[ON CAMERA]

[SERIOUS TONE]

Section five: Guardrails. This is the part most income-growth advice leaves out, and it matters just as much as the rest.

Four checkboxes. Check each one honestly.

This plan does not sacrifice my family time. This plan does not replace rest. This plan is rooted in stewardship, not greed. And -- if this resonates with you -- I have prayed about this plan.

If you can't check all four, go back and adjust your plan. More money isn't worth it if it costs you your relationships, your health, or your integrity.

[PAUSE]

[ON CAMERA]

[WARM/ENCOURAGING]

Here are your action items for this week. Complete this 90-Day Income Map. Practice daily gratitude to combat comparison -- when you feel the trap pulling you in, name three specific things you're grateful for. Identify one skill you could monetize in the next ninety days. And have a conversation about growth opportunities at your current job. Just one conversation. That's all.

You don't need to change everything this week. You need to take one step. One step in the right direction is worth more than a thousand good intentions.

See you tomorrow.
